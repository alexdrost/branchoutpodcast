## Why this episode

You already know you should be building relationships, and you keep trading them away for whatever is due Friday. This episode is for people who work inside transactions, where the counterparties are adversarial by design, the hours are unpredictable, and burnout shows up before you will admit it. Gary Lewis makes the case that the fix is not a better calendar but a different posture toward the people already sitting in front of you.

## Guest

Gary Lewis is founder and managing partner of Aquila Equity Partners, a private investment firm based in Metro Detroit. He has worked from an operating seat, a private investing seat and an advisory seat, and he spent years in middle-market investment banking alongside host Alex Drost — which is why this conversation runs more like two colleagues comparing scars than an interview. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/garynlewis/).

## Key ideas

**Networking is a fundamental part of the job, not an errand you schedule around it.** Lewis argues it happens constantly and mostly unnoticed, including inside your own office. Your colleagues today are your ex-colleagues tomorrow, and almost nobody spends forty years at one firm anymore.

**The people across the table on a deal are the richest network you have access to.** Opposing counsel, the buyer, the other side's accountants — Lewis says you see more of them than you do your own teammates. Treat the negotiation as binary and combative and somebody ends up upset; treat it as fair and it outlasts the deal.

**Work-life integration is the honest frame, and balance is not.** This is the point Lewis flags as controversial before he makes it. Businesses do not stop because it is five o'clock on a Friday, so the workable model is integration punctuated by a handful of true shutdowns a year — the ones where the phone goes on vacation too.

**Burnout is a communication failure as much as a workload failure.** Lewis describes getting frustrated with teammates, clients and portfolio companies while he was burning out, and then realizing he was blaming others for his own reaction. What made stepping back survivable was telling people in advance what he was doing and why.

**Build the outlet before you need it.** A nonprofit board, a softball team, biking — Lewis frames these as recovery and as a network channel at the same time, and as something a manager finds very hard to say no to when the conversation happens openly.

**Rotating seats beats committing early to one declared career path.** Having sat in operating, investing and advisory roles is what Lewis credits for his empathy, his perspective and his credibility with people across the table. His advice to his younger self is to explore and accept that not every outcome has to be ideal.

## Try this

- On your current deal, name the three people on the other side you would want to work with again. After close, send one of them a note that asks for nothing.
- Put one genuine shutdown on the calendar this quarter. Before it starts, tell your team and your two most active clients the dates, the reason, and who to call instead.
- Join one thing outside work that meets on a schedule you do not control — a board seat, a league, a standing ride — so that stepping away is a commitment rather than a decision you re-make every week.
