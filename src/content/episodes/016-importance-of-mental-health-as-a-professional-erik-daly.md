## Why this episode

You have been tired for a long time and you have decided that is simply what the job costs. This episode is for professionals who have started to suspect that the only way out of a role that is grinding them down is out of the profession entirely. Erik Daly came close to making that call, and walks through what he did instead — including the parts that did not work.

## Guest

Erik Daly is a corporate and M&A attorney with Miller Johnson, a West Michigan-based law firm. He started practicing in Chicago in 2007, at the tail end of the last private equity boom, and moved his family to Grand Rapids several years later. His mother is a psychiatrist, which he offers as the reason the vocabulary of mental health came easily to him and does not to most attorneys. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/erik-daly-37532332/).

## Key ideas

**Physical symptoms are often the first observable sign that something is out of balance mentally.** Daly developed chronic back pain, fatigue and weakness in his mid to late twenties as a deal attorney, and explained them away as what happens when you sit in a chair reading documents. In retrospect he reads them as the first manifestation of the stress and depression underneath.

**There is no exit ramp, and expecting one sets you up to fail.** The move from Chicago to Grand Rapids genuinely improved his work-life balance. It also cured nothing, and Daly is careful to say that a dramatic change will not make everything clear.

**Job crafting is the real alternative to walking away.** After his father's death in 2016, Daly seriously considered leaving law. What he did instead was change the shape of the work — new skills, becoming a resource for other attorneys, intellectually interesting side avenues — and he credits colleagues who were willing to let him spend time on it.

**Concealment degrades your judgment, not just your mood.** Daly's argument for being open is not primarily about comfort. When you hide what is going on, you start projecting worst-case scenarios onto circumstances instead of seeing them as they are, and the decisions everyone is relying on get worse.

**The intervention does not have to be big to count.** Daly pushes back on the high-achiever framing — the ultramarathons, the 500-pound squats — and argues the first small increment carries most of the payoff. Ten or fifteen minutes of exercise in the morning, or five minutes of breathing at your desk, is a legitimate starting point.

**There is a business case a manager can accept without engaging the psychological one.** Attrition is expensive, and the cost of losing people you have invested in is high. Daly offers that as a standalone reason to pay attention, alongside the personal one.

## Try this

- Set a ten-minute floor tomorrow morning — stretch, move, breathe — and do not raise it until you have held it for two weeks. The point is the streak, not the session.
- Write down the physical thing your body has been doing for months that you have stopped noticing, then write down what changed at work in the same period. Treat the two columns as related until proven otherwise.
- Book thirty minutes with the person on your team who is most obviously buried, and ask how they are actually doing without mentioning their workload once.
