## Why this episode

You sit on a diversity committee, or you fund one, and a year of heritage-month programming has moved nothing. Brandon Hawkins argues that if you hold a C-suite title, the committee was never the lever — your next three hiring decisions are. This is for people with the authority to hire, recommend and refer, who have been treating diversity as a communications problem.

## Guest

Brandon Hawkins is Chief Revenue Officer at CFO Hub, an outsourced financial services firm working with middle-market companies, based in San Diego. He started in banking at Wells Fargo in January 1990, spent about thirteen years there, and later led the private banking group for City National Bank's de novo San Diego office — in his words, "one of one" in nearly every institution he worked in. He has coached high school football for twenty seasons and sits on ACG's DEI committee. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/igetthingsdone/).

## Key ideas

**Aggregating dates is not the work; hiring is the work.** Hawkins has no objection to Kwanzaa on the calendar or historic dates in the newsletter. His objection is that organizations like ACG are made of decision makers who can hire, recommend and refer, and are instead measuring themselves on programming.

**Slow progress is not a power problem — the power is already sitting in the room unspent.** He points out his father and grandfather had these same conversations. What is different now is that he and his peers hold the titles, which makes the pace a choice rather than a constraint.

**Minority leaders hesitate to hire minorities because they fear being implicated in a shared failure.** This is the uncomfortable one, and Hawkins names it directly: if the hire does not work out, the worry is that it reflects on everyone who looks like them. Meanwhile, he notes, the majority population hires on nepotism and good looks every day without that calculation.

**The question he cannot answer is procedural, and being diverse himself does not solve it.** How do you recruit from a community — he uses LGBTQ as the example — without asking in a way that is discriminatory or offensive? He admits he struggles with it, and that his seat on a DEI committee does not supply the answer.

**A homogeneous advisory team is badly matched to a middle-market client base that got there by nontraditional routes.** His clients are the engineer from Harvard who now designs women's apparel. Professional services runs a narrow, standardized funnel into a client population that ran no such funnel at all — and Hawkins says prospects notice the mismatch, share information more openly when they don't see it, and stick around longer.

**On the revenue side, coachability and scrappiness beat the resume, and he has been burned proving it.** Technical credentials matter on the accounting side — CPA, public accounting, time in a private company. On the sales side he says he has failed repeatedly by hiring people who were perfect on paper and were not good humans.

## Try this

- Name one open role at another firm in your network this week and refer a candidate into it who would not have found the posting. Recommending and referring costs you nothing and does not require your own headcount.
- On your next hire, put your HR or DEI lead in the room as an equal vote rather than a resume screen. Hawkins draws the line at final decision maker, not at participation.
- For your next revenue hire, delete the school and firm-pedigree filter from the screen and replace it with two questions about coachability and hustle. Keep the technical credential screen where it genuinely applies.
