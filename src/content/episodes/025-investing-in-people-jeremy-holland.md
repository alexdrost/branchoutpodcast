## Why this episode

You have thousands of names in the CRM and you cannot remember meeting most of them. Jeremy Holland runs originations for a private equity firm that has made more than 650 investments, and he would trade the entire database for eighty people who would run through a wall for him. This is for the deal professional trying to decide where the next hour of relationship time should go, and it comes with the mechanics attached rather than the sentiment.

## Guest

Jeremy Holland is Managing Partner of Originations for The Riverside Company, a global middle market private equity and structured capital provider that has made more than 650 investments. His stated view of his own role is that he invests in people rather than in businesses, and the episode is largely him showing his work: how he delivers a pass, how he handles a colleague who has just lost a process, how he treats someone who has been out of the industry for a year. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/jeremyrholland/).

## Key ideas

**Depth beats breadth, and Holland puts a number on the trade.** Eighty people in the industry who have invested in your career and have a stake in your success are worth more than 5,000 business cards from conferences you cannot recall. Drost pushes back that you still need continuous new-relationship flow, much of it one conversation deep.

**Private candor is the caring option, and withholding it is not kind.** Holland's phrase is that friends stab friends in the front: name the issue directly, privately, politely, so the person can correct it. The examples are small and invisible to their owner - a habit of eye-rolling, speech littered with filler words, a Zoom camera pointed up the nose.

**A pass is an opportunity to add value rather than a negative call.** The tweak is offering a warm introduction to a firm the opportunity actually fits. In an industry Holland describes as full of takers, an attempt to add to someone's process puts you in a different category even when they already knew the name.

**The moment a contact loses their job is when your investment is worth the most.** Most of their rolodex evaporates because people instantly compute that this person cannot give right now. Leaning in instead - a long lunch, a couple of hours building an action list, a text every few weeks so following up is never their job - is disproportionately valuable and remembered for years.

**Goodwill compounds over decades and can be impaired in five minutes.** A colleague venting at an investment banker after a second-place finish will cost the firm years of repair, and the departure of that colleague does not fix it. Rebuilding takes consistency of behavior across many settings over a long period.

**Losing gracefully is how you win the next one.** Riverside has won opportunities it originally lost by taking the bad news professionally and staying, in the industry phrase, hanging around the hoop. The discipline is getting off the hot call, venting to a colleague, and scheduling the post-mortem a week out.

## Try this

- The next time you pass on a deal, do not end the call at no. Name one buyer the opportunity actually fits and offer to make the introduction while you are still on the phone.
- Pick one person in your network who is between roles. Book a two-hour lunch this week, leave with a written list of people you will contact on their behalf, and set your own reminder to text them in three weeks so chasing you is never their job.
- Agree with one colleague that you are each other's vent. When a call turns hot, get off it, call them instead, and put the post-mortem with the other side on the calendar a week out.
