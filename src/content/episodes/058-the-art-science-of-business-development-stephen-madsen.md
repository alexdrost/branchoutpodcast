## Why this episode

You spend your week on calls, coffees, conferences and flights, and you have no idea which of those hours are actually producing anything. Stephen Madsen ran the numbers on two years of his own firm's deal flow and found the venues differ by an order of magnitude. This episode gives you a way to test your own habits instead of defending them.

## Guest

Stephen Madsen is Director of Business Development, Deal Origination and Capital Markets at Monomoy Capital Partners, a middle-market private equity firm investing in manufacturing, distribution and consumer products businesses. He co-authored the Middle Market Growth article "In Person Is the Essential Currency of PE Business Development," which is where this conversation started. He began building his network at 22, and the analysis he describes here rests on a watch-list system his team started in 2017. Find him at [linkedin.com/in/stephensmadsen](https://www.linkedin.com/in/stephensmadsen/).

## Key ideas

**Business development has a science underneath it, and almost nobody looks.** Madsen splits the work into art — being memorable, doing the right favor at the right time — and science, which is what emerges once you have run hundreds or thousands of interactions and can see patterns in them. The point of the science is not to replace judgment but to direct it.

**The favor is the unit of account, and it stops working the moment you keep score.** The advice Madsen got the night before his first job in October 2008 was to treat favors like a bank account and make deposits constantly. Some deposits never come back. In aggregate they change how people permanently see you, which is a different asset than a returned favor.

**Assuming two important people already know each other is how introductions die.** Madsen's example is a private lender and a middle-market PE friend who had never met; the introduction he made in 2011 still pays him in both directions. Nobody resents the question, and the goodwill accrues even when the answer is "we already work with them."

**Per interaction, city visits produced roughly five times the management presentations and ten times the letters of intent that calls did.** Conferences landed about halfway between. Leads added to the watch list only differed about 2x by venue — the gap widens the further down the funnel you look, which is why Madsen measures mid-funnel milestones rather than closes in a business with a sub-1% close rate.

**Madsen is careful about what the data can and cannot prove.** He offers theories — longer meetings, the "one other thing" someone mentions on your way out the door, the fact that someone on every multi-person Zoom is reading email — and then declines to claim causation. He also raises the possibility that the opportunity is higher quality *because* of how it was uncovered.

**The banks with the most resources are the ones you most wrongly assume will remember you.** Charting two years of deals received against in-person meetings with top-tier banks, Madsen found eight separate points where a change in meeting activity preceded a change in deal volume by one or two months, in both directions, with no seasonality.

## Try this

- Take the last ten opportunities that reached a real stage in your pipeline and write down, for each one, where the first conversation about it happened: a call, a video call, a conference, or a meeting in someone's office. If you cannot reconstruct it, that is the finding.
- Tier your top 30 contacts this week by deal size, relevance and internal influence, then set a contact cadence per tier and mark which tiers get an in-person touch. Put the cadence in your CRM so it prompts you instead of you remembering.
- Pick two people in your network you have assumed already know each other and ask one of them the question directly: "Have you two ever worked together?" Make the introduction if the answer is no.
