## Why this episode

You are walking into rooms where the person across the table has never bought from someone who looks like you, and the negotiation has already started before anyone speaks. Jamil Sanders, a Black advisor in wealth management, names the part that usually goes unsaid: the discount he applies to himself on the way in, and the specific structure he built to counter it. If you are not the person in that position, this is a direct account of what your endorsement is worth in your own network, and why he does not want your guilt.

## Guest

Jamil Sanders is Vice President and Relationship Manager with Key Bank in Cleveland, Ohio. He grew up reading The Wall Street Journal with a plan to get to Wall Street, was told by people who looked like him that there was no space in the industry, and built a private client practice in Cleveland instead. He is a returning guest — his first appearance covered achieving authenticity — and he serves on two boards and mentors young people outside of work. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/jamil-a-sanders/).

## Key ideas

**Sanders' first obstacle is not the client's bias but his own preemptive discount.** He describes going into a prospect meeting already telling himself, subliminally, that he is not good enough and that the other candidate will get chosen. His fix is sequential rather than motivational: establish the baseline that you are qualified before you work on anything the client can see.

**The discouragement came from inside his own community, and the screening came from outside it.** When Sanders asked people who looked like him about private banking, the answer was that there is no space for us in that industry. Then, once he was in, he found a second gate: prospects first register that he is not the typical advisor, then move to pedigree — where did you go to school — before anything about the work.

**Duality is not a choice between code-switching and unfiltered self-expression; Sanders is arguing for a negotiated middle.** He rejects living two lives, and he equally rejects treating authenticity as license to disregard what a client expects from an advisor. The target is a common ground he calls the sweet spot, which has to be found rather than declared.

**Diversify your mentors on purpose, because no single mentor supplies both things you need.** Sanders deliberately recruited one Black mentor and one white mentor. The first can say "this is the journey I've run" through cultural obstacles; the second, in his words, can supply rocket fuel — access to a CPA, an attorney, a network he has no route into.

**A colleague's explicit endorsement functions as a credential shortcut, and Sanders is specific about the mechanics.** When someone with access says to their circle "I really like this guy," the circle's first reaction is to ask why — and then to grant the benefit of the doubt. That gets Sanders past an initial screening he would otherwise have to survive on pedigree questions alone, after which his job is education, not qualification.

**He asks for fair equity, not preferential treatment, and he tells white colleagues that guilt is useless.** Sanders' argument is that guilt collapses into one of two failures: anger at being blamed for history you did not make, or a paralysis where you cannot find a place to start. What he wants instead is people acting inside their own circle of influence — talking to their network, their colleagues, their grandparents — which is what he means by all hands on deck.

## Try this

- Write down the two or three people you actually go to for career advice. If none of them has run the same race you are running, and none of them can put your name into a network you have no access to, you are missing one of Sanders' two mentors — go recruit that one this quarter.
- If you have access a colleague does not, send one introduction this week that names them and vouches for them to three specific people in your circle. Sanders' point is that the endorsement is what clears the screening; the meeting alone does not.
- Take Alex Drost's call to action literally: in the next seven days, get one meeting on the calendar with someone outside the circle you normally spend time in. Booking it counts even if it happens later.
