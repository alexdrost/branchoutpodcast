## Why this episode

Someone on your team is not performing and you have already decided the problem is them. Dan Cremons argues that the first place to look is your own mirror — and he means it literally, having once handed his executive team a stack of folders with a mirror inside each one. This is for managers who keep escalating people problems and for anyone whose own work has quietly stopped mattering to them.

## Guest

Dan Cremons spent roughly fifteen years in private equity, much of it with Alpine Investors, as an investor, board member, CEO and CXO in portfolio companies. He now runs his own firm working with private equity groups and their newly acquired portfolio companies on value creation planning and the human capital side of it. He is the author of The Blue Flame and Winning Moves, and he is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/dancremons/).

## Key ideas

**Disengagement usually means the role is wrong, not the person.** Cremons's Blue Flame conversation puts three questions on a whiteboard — what are you great at, what energizes you, what gives you meaning — and locates the person's current work against the intersection. When someone is struggling, the dot is almost always off center, and the fix is recrafting the role.

**When your team underperforms, the first diagnostic is the manager, not the personnel file.** Cremons noticed his executives arriving at one-on-ones with a KPI off track and an explanation that pointed at Mary or Steve. He put a mirror in each of their folders and kept one on his own desk from then on. The question that goes with it: what am I doing or not doing that is contributing to the very problem I'm trying to avoid?

**Servant leadership is also self-interested, and Cremons says so outright.** If you want to thrive as a leader, you have to take accountability for getting the people in your charge playing at a high level. That is not a contradiction of the altruistic framing — it is the reason the framing survives contact with a P&L.

**The framework found him before it found anyone else, and it cost him his career in private equity.** Writing the book during a 2020 summer sabbatical is what revealed his own flame was barely flickering. He left the industry rather than argue with the diagnosis — which is also the answer to whether the Blue Flame conversation must end in an internal reassignment. Sometimes it ends outside the organization.

**Young people are far more capable than the standard career ladder assumes.** Alpine's CEO-in-Training program has graduated people into portfolio CEO roles at an average age in the early 30s, running businesses with $25 to $50 million of EBITDA, against an industry default that a PE-grade CEO is fifty and has been around the block. The two screening attributes: adversity quotient and a growth mindset — a CEO of a company that has to grow 20% has to be growing 25%.

**Stretching someone only works if you override your instinct to rescue them.** Cremons's default is to jump in with the answer, and he treats that default as the thing to beat. Let them flail, accept the short-term delivery cost, and intervene only when the problem is a genuine existential risk to the business.

## Try this

- Before you escalate anyone's performance problem this week, write down your answer to Cremons's mirror question: what am I doing or not doing that is contributing to the very problem I'm trying to avoid? Do it in writing, not in your head.
- Book an uninterrupted hour with the person on your team you are most frustrated by, and spend it entirely on them. Draw the three lines — great at, energizing, meaningful — and mark where their current role sits relative to the middle. Do not discuss the performance issue until the hour is up.
- Build your own one-page plan this quarter, four columns: purpose and values, five-year vision and strategy, one-year goals, and no more than five to eight quarterly goals. Cremons runs relationships, physical and spiritual goals through the same format, and revisits it every quarter.
