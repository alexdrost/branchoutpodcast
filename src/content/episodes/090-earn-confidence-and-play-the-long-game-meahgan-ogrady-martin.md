## Why this episode

You are analytical, and it is costing you. Meahgan O'Grady Martin left quantitative valuation at Goldman with no investment banking on her resume, pitched an unpaid private equity internship to her own network, collected a stack of no's, and built a career on the assumption that she can figure things out. If you are stalled on a career decision, or telling yourself the reason you have not moved is something that happened to you, this is the episode that argues both are choices.

## Guest

Meahgan O'Grady Martin is Director of Originations at Palladium Equity Partners, where she leads the firm's originations effort. She began in the Derivatives Valuation Center at Ernst & Young, moved to Goldman for credit and liquid credit valuations on the high yield trading desk, and entered private equity through a self-pitched pre-MBA internship at Long Point Capital. She chairs the Association for Corporate Growth's Women in Leadership committee and was named one of McGuireWoods' Women to Know in Private Equity. ## Key ideas

**Confidence is earned and arrogance is given, and the earning is what supplies the humility.** O'Grady Martin's test is where the trait came from. Confidence that came out of discipline, training and difficulty carries an awareness of what you do not know; a person who appears to have arrived with it probably has the other thing. Her own version shows up as holding a researched position while staying open to a better one.

**Victimhood is pervasive, self-selected, and the specific thing that gets in the way of success.** She is blunt about it: sometimes you genuinely are the victim of a bad scenario, but continuing to be one is a decision. Alex Drost adds the second cost, which is comparison — measuring yourself against someone whose circumstances you cannot actually see burns the same energy for nothing.

**A pity party is allowed if you give it an end time.** This is her mother's rule, and she uses roughly thirty minutes. The point is not stoicism. It is that grief with no scheduled end runs indefinitely, and the people who move forward are not the ones nothing happened to.

**What you should be confident in is not the answer but your ability to find it.** She almost never said no early in her career, and credits that to knowing she would either figure it out or find the person who could. That reliance on other people is the operational reason she treats her network as infrastructure rather than a favor bank.

**A network treated as transactional dies at the next transaction.** Her alternative is deliberate giving — an introduction, a passed deal, plain advice — with a payoff measured in years. She is also unromantic about when it happens: nights, early mornings, lunch hours, and days that are therefore longer. There is no magic arrow, and she names The 4-Hour Work Week as a fallacy.

**Careers are marathons, and almost no decision is a one-way door.** Analysis paralysis costs highly analytical people more than a suboptimal choice does. Treat the next move as a stepping stone to the one after it, because that is what it is, and because even outcomes as final-sounding as bankruptcy are recoverable.

## Try this

- The next time something goes against you, set a thirty-minute timer, feel it fully, and write down the one action you will take when it goes off. Then take that action the same day.
- Pick the career decision you have been circling and write one sentence answering whether it is genuinely irreversible. If it is not, set a date this month by which you will decide, and hold it.
- Put three 45-minute conversations on next week's calendar outside working hours — early morning or lunch — and in each one lead with something you can give: an introduction, a name, a piece of information they do not have.
