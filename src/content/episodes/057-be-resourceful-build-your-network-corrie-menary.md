## Why this episode

You have been taught to spend networking time on people who might send you business, which writes off most of the room. Corrie Menary, a partner at a Cleveland private equity firm, says her single most effective network is her direct competitors, because they are working the same problems on the same Monday and some of them are further along. She is also unusually direct about the mid-career years when she let her network lapse, and about how much harder it was to pick the pieces back up.

## Guest

Corrie Menary is a Partner with Kirtland Capital Partners, a middle-market private equity firm in Cleveland, Ohio, in an organization of fewer than ten people. Because the firm is small, she is often the team rather than the person assigning it, and the work in the companies they buy is basic: finding the right business insurance, or giving a cash-only business a way to invoice and take cards. Her LinkedIn profile photo is a Swiss Army knife, which is close to how she describes the job. Find her at [linkedin.com/in/corrie-menary-private-equity-](https://www.linkedin.com/in/corrie-menary-private-equity-/).

## Key ideas

**In small-business private equity the product is resourcefulness, not sophisticated value creation.** Menary's examples are business insurance and a transactional system for a company that ran entirely on cash. What she sells a seller is a network capable of solving whichever problem shows up on a given day.

**Direct competitors are the most effective network she has.** They face identical problems and are sometimes further along a particular journey. Her Cleveland framing is that a visibly active local deal community is what makes investment banks want to bring deals to the region, which she values above any single deal she loses to a neighbor.

**Owners of small businesses have nobody to say the frightening thing to.** They cannot tell their own leadership team that capacity has run out or that a shipment from China now costs ten times more without spreading anxiety through the workforce. Being the phone-a-friend is real value delivered before any capital moves.

**She neglected her network during the associate and VP years, and says restarting is harder than maintaining.** Most guests present their networking as continuous. Menary does not, and she now tells younger professionals to treat it as part of the job rather than something owed to the evening.

**The belief that networking has to happen outside business hours is self-imposed.** Her own version of it was a fear of being seen internally as not doing the rest of her job. A midday ACG event or a lunch is working time, and a five-minute note remembering that someone's daughter started kindergarten does more than a quarterly check-in.

**Most people you meet will never send you a deal, and the return still arrives.** A lender's offhand referral produced an associate who was not looking for a job. Calls to accounting firms whose clients buy a target's product produced customer diligence she would otherwise have paid for.

## Try this

- Ask an owner or a client what is keeping them from growing at twice the rate they grew last year, using a number they actually hit. Menary uses it to get past the polished answer and into the real constraint.
- Book one lunch inside working hours this month with a direct competitor, and bring a problem you are genuinely stuck on rather than a pitch.
- Join one room that is not your industry's room. Menary's example is a local Chamber of Commerce, chosen precisely because it is not accountants, lawyers, bankers and private equity people.
