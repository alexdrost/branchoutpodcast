## Why this episode

You lose a competitive pitch, a prospect goes quiet, and the whole thing is out of your hands by the time you hear about it. Tom Boniface argues that the part still in your hands is bigger than it looks, and that it gets decided on an ordinary Tuesday rather than in the big moments. This is for the professional early in a business development role who is waiting for a break instead of managing the five variables they actually control.

## Guest

Tom Boniface is Vice President of Business Development for Hillco Global, which he joined three years before this recording. He was a college swimmer - an individual medley specialist - and he pulls most of his process discipline from how that training worked: find the weak stroke in October for the race in March. Every year he sits down with his boss, the head of his group and the four or five colleagues who see his daily work and asks them where he is weak. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/tom-boniface-67186522/).

## Key ideas

**Outcomes are dictated by your response to the event, not by the event.** Boniface runs his career on the R Factor, a framework from Tim Kight and Urban Meyer. Cancer diagnosis or job promotion, the event lands either way; the response is the only input you own, and the daily version of it is small.

**Blame, complain and defend is the response that ends a business development career.** When a competitor takes a deal, the framework gives you two doors: BCD, or ownership and a lesson you can apply next time. Boniface applies the same test to a prospect declining a coffee.

**Reflection is worthless without brutal honesty and self-responsibility.** Naming your own flaws as growth opportunities is the precondition for improving on them, and Boniface treats that naming as a positive act rather than a confession.

**You cannot see your own weaknesses, so recruit people who will tell you.** His mechanism is an annual conversation with his boss and his group head asking directly what they see as his weaknesses, the same question put to the four peers who watch him work, and a wife he describes as extremely honest. Reflection then becomes the work of weeding through the fluff to find the truth in what they said.

**Copying the rainmaker partner's networking style is leaning into your weakness.** Boniface knows he is better one-on-one, so he optimizes for fewer and deeper conversations while a colleague works the whole room. Drost, who is the fifty-handshake type, agrees from the other side: there are a million ways to accomplish the same goal.

**Staying partly connected on vacation makes him more present, not less.** He is explicit that this is what works for him rather than a rule for anyone else. A week behind on email turns him into someone unpleasant to be around, which costs his family and his business more than the twenty minutes of inbox costs the vacation.

## Try this

- Break business development into its parts and give each one its own calendar block this week - outreach calls, follow-up notes, research time. Boniface schedules note-taking separately from calling. Treat those blocks the way you treat a client deadline.
- Book thirty minutes with your group head in the next two weeks and ask one question: where am I weak, and how do I move forward. Ask the same of four colleagues who see your daily work, then look for the answer that shows up more than once.
- Rewrite your task list so every item is something your own action can complete. Twenty calls a day stays on. Get promoted comes off, and gets replaced by the calls.
