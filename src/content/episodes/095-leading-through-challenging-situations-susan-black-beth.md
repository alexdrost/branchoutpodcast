## Why this episode

You have been in the room where the facts changed twice before lunch and people were waiting on you anyway. Susan Black-Beth describes what she actually did in those rooms — leading multi-unit franchise businesses through COVID, then, in November 2020, making medical decisions about her twelve-year-old son after a massive heart attack. Roughly a third of this conversation is that second story, told in her own words. What she draws from both is a leadership posture you can copy: calm as an output, decisions stated as provisional, and gratitude worked at deliberately rather than felt.

## Guest

Susan Black-Beth is Chief Operating Officer of Avante Capital Partners. She grew up in her parents' business, the largest self-serve carwash chain in the country, bought her first location with her sister at nineteen, and spent about fourteen years converting the family licensing model into a franchise system. She then spent seven years as COO of a private equity fund focused on franchise and multi-unit businesses, and founded Team Up Advisory Services before joining Avante full time after Labor Day 2022. She also speaks professionally. [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/susan-e-blackbeth/)

## Key ideas

**In an acute crisis the leader's deliverable is calm, and it does not have to be genuine.** Black-Beth is direct that you fake it until you make it, that the fear goes home with you rather than to the team, and that the reason is arithmetic: tens, hundreds or thousands of people are counting on you to figure it out. She scopes this narrowly to crisis. On an ordinary hard day, she says her team will know it, especially if it affects the work.

**Say out loud that you are deciding with the facts you have and that those facts will change.** This is the sentence she used repeatedly during COVID, when guidance moved at midnight. Naming the provisional quality up front is what let her make calls quickly without spending credibility every time one had to be reversed.

**Name the culture in plain language and then hold people to it.** In a shop of four to eight people she defined what she called the firm's defining characteristics: be a good partner to our partners, whoever the partner in the sentence is, and — in an industry she describes as full of sharp elbows — none at our table. She still counts those colleagues as friends.

**COVID was a different category of problem because the fear was personal, not financial.** People who would charge the hill with you had a sick seventy-six-year-old at home. Her point is that no textbook and no leadership training covered that variable, so it came down to character rather than method. It is also why she says ego had to be checked at the door, and why direct competitors in franchising sat down together.

**The private equity habit of measuring and mitigating risk transferred to decisions that were not about money.** After her son's transplant, a second clot forced a choice between operating that night while he was on blood thinners or waiting three days for them to burn off. She and her husband waited. She credits the analytical discipline she built in investing with making that a decision rather than a panic.

**Silver linings are a discipline, because the negative arrives on its own.** She pushes back on describing the pandemic as the worst two and a half years of anyone's life: it broke a cycle of a hundred hotel nights a year she had not managed to break herself. Gratitude, in her framing, does not require pretending the hard thing was not hard.

## Try this

- In your next hard update, open with the sentence itself: this is the best decision I can make with the facts I have today, and those facts will change. Then say what would cause you to change it.
- Write down two or three defining characteristics for your team in words a new hire would understand, and read them out at your next team meeting. Hers were "be a good partner to our partners" and "no sharp elbows at our table."
- If you sit on a board or invest in operators, call one CEO this week with no agenda and say explicitly that you are available as a friend, not as a board member. She built her advisory practice around how few people have that call available.
