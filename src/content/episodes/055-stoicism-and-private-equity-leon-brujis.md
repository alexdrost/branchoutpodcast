## Why this episode

You work somewhere that explains its good outcomes with skill and its bad ones with conditions. Leon Brujis, a private equity partner, argues that asymmetry is the industry's real accountability problem, and he demonstrates the alternative on himself — walking through a deal he talked himself into while on a hot streak. If you are in a business where admitting you do not know something feels expensive, this is a direct case that the silence costs more.

## Guest

Leon Brujis is a Partner at Palladium Equity Partners, a New York based private equity firm, where he has been investing for about 15 years. He came to Stoicism sideways: he met General Mattis, read Call Sign Chaos, and followed its reading list to Marcus Aurelius' Meditations. He is candid in this episode that he entered the industry as a taker and that the habit of asking gotcha questions held his early career back. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/lbrujis/).

## Key ideas

**Brujis says he came into private equity as a taker, and that the industry's reputation problem starts there rather than in its communications.** People are drawn by money and pedigree, and the power to reshape companies encourages a masters-of-the-universe posture. His argument is not that this behavior fails — plenty of takers do very well — but that it carries a cost that public scrutiny eventually collects.

**Treating counterparties well is not a tax on returns.** Brujis' claim is the opposite: deeper partnerships with colleagues and management teams get more done, so the ethical position and the performance position point the same direction. This is the load-bearing claim of the episode, and he is clear it is a belief he arrived at rather than started with.

**The failure of accountability is an attribution asymmetry, and the fix is a sentence.** When a deal works, the thesis was right and the management team was right. When it does not, everyone becomes a victim of a market that turned. Brujis' prescription is to say plainly that the investment was bad and that you screwed up.

**Failure is a ladder rather than a weight, and Palladium has an adage for it.** Good decisions come from experience, and experience comes from bad decisions. Brujis applies it to his own case: on a run of successful transactions, he built a thesis on a hyped, competitive process and rationalized real supply chain and cyclicality warning signs as manageable because the playbook had worked before.

**Optimize for repetitions, not for one perfect attempt.** His clay analogy: give one person two days to make the perfect pot and another instructions to make as many as possible, and the volume maker produces the better pot, because each one teaches the next. His related line — you do not learn to swim by reading about swimming — is aimed at an apprenticeship business where the lesson arrives through the work.

**The stigma around not knowing is specific to this industry and it stunts people.** Brujis says he was afraid of looking stupid early on, did not ask enough questions, and that it came back to haunt him. What replaced it was Simon Sinek's be-the-idiot-in-the-room posture and a rule about intent: listen with the intent to listen, not with the intent to respond. His measure of success, drawn from the goodbye video his father's IFC team made him, is whether people would line up to say you made a difference.

## Try this

- At your next post-mortem on something that went badly, write one sentence naming the decision you got wrong before you write any sentence about the market or the conditions. Then say that sentence out loud in the room.
- Prepare your next conversation with a senior colleague or a prospective employer the way Brujis prepared for this one: lead with the two things that did not work and what you took from them, rather than your best transactions.
- In your next deal or client meeting, ask the question you were planning to sit on because it might make you look uninformed. Brujis' bet is that it is the question most people in the room already have.
