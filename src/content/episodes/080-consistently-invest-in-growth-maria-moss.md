## Why this episode

You know development matters and it still keeps sliding off the calendar, usually in the weeks you need it most. Maria Moss argues the failure is structural rather than motivational: development is piece-by-piece work that has to be automated into reminders and check-ins, because nothing about ordinary life will make room for it. She also makes the case that the highest-return moment to spend on yourself is the one right after you get fired.

## Guest

Maria Moss is a training and development consultant and owner of Phoenix Leadership Solutions, where she runs training, coaching and mentoring programs for organizations. She studied criminal justice and worked as a probation officer, kept being asked to train colleagues wherever she landed, and eventually moved into leadership development at a global company for about five years before a layoff pushed her into consulting six years ago. She has been fired once and laid off once, and treats both as the pivots that produced her business. Find her at [linkedin.com/in/maria-moss](https://www.linkedin.com/in/maria-moss/).

## Key ideas

**Training gets cut first because leaders cannot see its value, and the deferred bill is larger.** Moss went into corporate knowing her function was the first one affected in any reduction. Her counter-argument is that people are the only asset that makes an organization distinctive, and that if you do not build their ability to work together you will spend the money on replacing them instead. She reaches for Stephen Covey's line about being too busy driving to stop for gas.

**Development is piece-by-piece and has to be automated, because life will otherwise win.** Her framing is the old joke about getting an elephant into a refrigerator. The practical translation is calendar reminders to reread your notes, subscriptions to coaching tools, newsletters — anything that re-engages you with a skill without requiring you to remember to.

**An assessment result is a baseline, not a verdict.** Moss's first emotional intelligence assessment came back badly enough that her reaction was there is no way I am this person. The reframe she now gives everyone she assesses is that the score is not good or bad, it is information, and that development is not a repair job on something broken.

**Measure growth qualitatively, months apart, or the measurement itself will discourage you.** Weighing yourself weekly, she points out, mostly teaches you to stop weighing yourself. The equivalent check-ins for soft skills are specific and behavioral: does that email still get under your skin, does the car cutting you off still set you off, do those particular relationships look different a year on.

**You judge yourself by your intentions while everyone else is only watching your actions.** That gap is Moss's argument for an accountability partner — someone you trust enough to ask how you have actually been handling things, who can give you a read your own self-assessment cannot.

**The moment you least want to invest in yourself is the one that pays.** Moss got fired with a baby on the way, and the question that changed her trajectory was not what her boss did wrong but what she contributed. After the later layoff she gave herself an explicit four-week window to be miserable in, then enrolled in free classes at a local entrepreneur center and started writing a business plan. She is also clear that the cost is not the barrier: books, podcasts and free workshops count.

## Try this

- Open the notes from the last course, workshop or conference you paid for and put a recurring reminder in your calendar to reread them. Moss's claim is that the material was never the problem; the re-engagement is, and it does not survive being left to memory.
- Write down two specific reactions you want to be different — a particular sender's emails, a particular meeting, a particular person — and put a check-in ninety days out to reread what you wrote. Do not score yourself weekly. That is the scale, and it only produces discouragement.
- Ask one person who sees you every week how you have been handling the thing you are working on. You have access to your intentions and they have access to your behavior, and only one of those predicts how it is landing.
