## Why this episode

You cannot hire your way out of your capacity problem, and the requisition you sent HR is not going to be filled. Gary Shamis ran a CPA firm for 27 years, took it from a startup to about $100 million and 575 people, and sold it to BDO, and he now argues the managing partner's job is orchestrating capacity from five levers rather than one. If you are running a firm and deciding whether to merge, this episode makes the case for staying independent and tells you what to do instead.

## Guest

Gary Shamis is Principal of Winding River Consulting, a boutique advisory firm outside Cleveland working mostly with accounting firms on strategy. He was managing partner of a CPA firm for 27 years, growing it to roughly 575 people and about $100 million in revenue and the 37th largest firm in the country before selling to BDO, which he says was the largest transaction in their history. He runs the Managing Partner Bootcamp, now in its fourteenth iteration with about 120 participants. Find him at [linkedin.com/in/gary-shamis-wrc](https://www.linkedin.com/in/gary-shamis-wrc/).

## Key ideas

**The hundred-year accounting model is out of fuel, and capacity is now the binding constraint on growth.** Shamis describes the old machine plainly: hire young people, build a pyramid, make money on leverage. The quantity and quality of that human capital is no longer available, so growth is capped by capacity to do the work rather than by demand.

**Shamis names five levers and says the managing partner conducts all of them.** Hiring and retention, process improvement, technology, practice management that culls wrong-fit clients, and outsourcing. Handing a headcount request to HR is no longer on the list, because that order will not get filled.

**Never let the person who built a process be the one who analyzes it.** His alternative is concrete: trade with a peer firm, send your tax lead to review their practice and have theirs review yours. He also thinks there is an unserved business in doing this work, because almost nobody does.

**Culling clients fails because partner comp is built on the book of business.** Shamis is direct that practice management gets executed badly for structural reasons, not lazy ones — partners have no incentive to give clients up. His own version was the 10% rule: raise rates 10% expecting to lose 10% of clients, then find that most people will not switch accountants.

**Shamis's contrarian call is that the merger frenzy is herd behavior in a market that just opened up.** Sarbanes-Oxley pushed the Big Four upmarket, the next tier of billion-dollar firms followed them, and he thinks that leaves the middle market to whoever wants it. He grants what BDO gave him — international tax capability — and points out alliances can supply the same thing without the merger.

**Above roughly $10 million in revenue, the managing partner should have exactly one client: the firm.** He did this in 1999, gave up the largest book in the firm, and grew from $12 million to $100 million over the next 15 years. To de-risk it for someone else, he suggests guaranteeing that partner's compensation at an agreed level for two or three years with a right to rebuild their practice.

## Try this

- Call a managing partner at a comparable firm and propose a swap: your tax lead reviews their process, theirs reviews yours, both report back in writing within 60 days.
- Write down the parameters that disqualify an acquisition target for you — no succession plan, partner ages that mismatch your client base — then have someone research and list the specific firms that pass, and approach two of them.
- Take your client list and mark every relationship you would not accept today. Decide which ones survive because of a related engagement and which are simply legacy, then raise rates on the legacy group at the next renewal.
