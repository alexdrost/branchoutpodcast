## Why this episode

You are months into something you expected to last weeks, and the energy you started with has run out. Kurt Wilkin has been through the dot-com collapse with a new baby and no job, through 2008, and through the year this was recorded, and his answer is not to be more positive about it. It is the Stockdale paradox: unbreakable faith that you will get through, held at the same time as an honest accounting of how bad the present actually is. That pairing is deliberately not the same thing as optimism, which Wilkin argues is what breaks people first.

## Guest

Kurt Wilkin is co-founder and chief evangelist of HireBetter. After the dot-com bubble burst he lost his job, had a new baby and a wife who had just left Ernst & Young, and started consulting to make ends meet — which became a $20 million professional services firm he later sold to Tatum CFO. The week before this recording he stepped aside as HireBetter's CEO and handed the role to his partner. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/kurt-wilkin-7345255/).

## Key ideas

**Quitting is the thing to rule out. Failure is not.** Drost offers "failure is not an option" and Wilkin corrects him on the spot. He failed repeatedly during the years he was building that first firm, made some right adjustments and some wrong ones, and the only commitment that held was refusing to stop.

**Optimists fare worst in a long crisis.** Wilkin takes the frame from Jim Stockdale by way of Jim Collins: the prisoners who kept predicting release by Christmas were the ones who died after Christmas, deflated. He watched the same curve in the CEOs he met in June and July, who had sprinted through what they assumed was a twelve-week disruption and hit a wall when it turned out to be a marathon.

**Secure survival first, then use the disruption offensively.** Wilkin's sequence is explicit. Batten the hatches, extend the cash runway, and only then treat the crisis as cover for the changes you needed to make anyway — personnel decisions, the business model, the retail lease you wanted out of.

**Being watched is what manufactures the courage.** Wilkin says knowing his family, his team and his clients are all watching forces him to be braver than he actually is. The hiding-in-the-closet posture is simply not available once you hold a leadership position, and he treats that constraint as useful rather than unfair.

**The founder is not automatically the right CEO.** Wilkin had been contemplating stepping aside for years and used the year's disruption as the occasion to actually do it. His self-assessment is that he is a good founder and a good evangelist, so the correct move was to put himself where he is strong and get out of his partner's way.

**Reaching out in a crisis is for community; the sales come later or not at all.** Asked for the single lesson, Wilkin lands on connecting with others, and immediately rules out doing it in order to sell. You do not sell without a relationship, and serving the peer group he was in is what got him through as much as it got them through.

## Try this

- Write down the two facts about your current situation you have been declining to say out loud, and next to them the one outcome you still believe you will reach. Keep them on the same page. That pairing is the whole of what Wilkin means by resilience.
- Block two hours away from the desk tomorrow and take one of your calls while walking. Wilkin's rule is that it does not matter whether those hours land between 8:00 and 5:00 — the work will be there either way.
- If you lead people, tell one person to take Friday off and not call in, then take one yourself within the month. Wilkin admits he is not the best example here, and Drost's point is that an instruction you visibly ignore teaches the opposite lesson.
