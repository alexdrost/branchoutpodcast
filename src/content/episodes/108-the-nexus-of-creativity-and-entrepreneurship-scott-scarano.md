## Why this episode

You built the firm, and somewhere around year twelve you stopped being able to say what the growth was for. Scott Scarano ran his CPA firm for fifteen years, used EOS to work himself out of operations and sales until only the visionary seat was left, and then found himself bored — which is how he ended up recording a podcast and writing accounting rap parodies. If you have been treating creative work as something other people do for a living, this is an hour on why the entrepreneurial act was already it.

## Guest

Scott Scarano built and grew his own CPA firm for fifteen years, planning his exit from operations and sales through EOS until the visionary seat was the only one he occupied. He now runs Accounting High and has recorded over 200 episodes, and has written twelve rap parodies, including the Eminem parody for Expensify's CEO that closes this episode. He is direct about the earlier part: he learned business selling drugs in college, got arrested, and went back to finish an accounting degree. [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/scottscarano/)

## Key ideas

**Nobody creates anything genuinely new, and that is the point.** Scarano's definition of creativity is finding a unique way, from your own worldview, to bridge things that already exist. Running a firm is that same act — new pathways, new combinations, under conditions where no one has told you the answer.

**The people who claim the label "creative" are usually the least creative.** He credits the line to David Barrett, who said it on his show, and takes it seriously: if creation is universal, then sorting people into creatives and non-creatives mostly tells you who has permission to try. Knowledge workers building a firm qualify whether or not they would use the word.

**Skill is invisible day to day, so trust the reps rather than the feeling.** Scarano describes a curve — a "peak of mount stupid" where you think you can handle any return that comes in, a valley after you break something badly, then a slow climb back. His mechanism for getting through it was editing his own early episodes so he had to hear his own mistakes before he hired an editor.

**A no is usually about timing, not about the idea.** The Accountant Bracket Challenge sat in the back of a notebook for a year. When Expensify declined to attach a giveaway to it he nearly abandoned the whole thing, and the people around him kept going anyway. The tournament ran longer than tax season and drew about 2,000 people.

**Growth for its own sake was fifteen years without a reason.** He is blunt that growing the firm was the only thing he cared about and that he could not say why beyond wanting more money. The visionary seat he engineered his way into then left him with nothing to do while the firm just needed stability through COVID.

**Indifference is the failure mode, not criticism.** Told by another host that he was overworking a rap and the sponsor would like it fine as it was, Scarano refused: liking it was not the target. He says he does not fear a negative reaction — a negative reaction means the work registered.

## Try this

- Start a file of ideas that got a no, with the date and the stated reason. Reread it every quarter and mark which reasons were about timing rather than about the idea.
- Before you make the next thing, take apart one piece of work you admire. Scarano mapped Eminem's rhyme schemes and read two or three years of a CEO's public emails before writing a word of the parody.
- Ship the next ten reps of whatever you are learning without judging the output, and do your own editing at least at the start, so you are forced to hear your own mistakes.
