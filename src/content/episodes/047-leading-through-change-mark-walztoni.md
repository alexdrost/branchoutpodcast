## Why this episode

You have a team that is technically fine and quietly unproductive, and a change underway that everyone has already decided to wait out. Mark Walztoni spends this hour on the two things most leaders get backwards: that the friction phase is a required stage you should be announcing before it starts, and that a project everyone found pleasant is evidence you did not lead it. He works on merger integrations, so the examples come with real stakes attached.

## Guest

Mark Walztoni is a Managing Director with Crowe, based in the firm's Grand Rapids, Michigan office. His practice covers M&A, HR due diligence and integration, which means he is usually in the room when two organizations have to become one and nobody has explained why. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/markwalztoni/) and checks his messages there.

## Key ideas

**Management owns the things; leadership owns the how and the why.** Walztoni's example is the return-to-office problem. Desk reservations, mask rules and logistics are a management function and a good one. The leadership function is the video he watched from a near-retirement executive who told two thousand people he expected a victory lap and is instead picking Cheerios out of a car seat because his daughter's family moved in. Both jobs are required, often from different people on the same team.

**Serving a stakeholder and pleasing one are different jobs, and the difference is where leadership actually lives.** Pleasing means reflecting back what they said and designing to the boss's stated vision. Serving means asking the questions that make the vision better and being ready to advocate a position constructively. Walztoni's test is blunt: if the project ends and everyone feels pleased, you are probably not getting another leadership assignment. What you want to hear afterward is that it was painful and they are glad it went that way.

**Tell the team the conflict phase is coming before it arrives, then keep talking about it during and after.** Storming is a stage, not a symptom. Walztoni's first instruction to a leader is to name it in advance and say plainly that when it shows up it is not a reason to worry. Alex extends it into a cadence: before, so nobody is surprised; during, so discomfort reads as normal rather than as failure; after, so the team gets faster at it next time.

**Change is a date. Transition is everything after it, and the muddy middle is where the work happens.** A wedding, a birth, a go-live — each is a point in time. Walztoni calls the period that follows wandering in the wilderness, and says it is where storming actually occurs. He also flags "new normal" as a derailer, because it promises a stable endpoint that history says is not coming.

**Team formation has to be run from the enterprise, not from your department.** The best available person plays their best available spot, even when the enterprise takes your strongest project manager so she can learn a new area and a junior steps up. Walztoni is honest that the first reaction is "sounds like all loss." He also found, in a merger integration, a leader parked in a gray niche role who turned out to have run a division and founded a company — an undiscovered resource sitting in the grandstands.

**Executives derail on overused strengths, not on weaknesses.** The senior executive Walztoni describes could improve anything from 95% to 99% and had built a habit of proving it. His own conclusion was that doing so made him roughly half as effective, because everyone else stopped bringing things up. The corrective Walztoni was given: resist the temptation to tell people everything you know.

## Try this

- At your next project kickoff, say out loud that a conflict phase is coming, roughly when to expect it, and that it is not a sign anything went wrong. Put it in the kickoff deck so you are held to repeating it when it lands.
- Plan six deliveries in six formats for your next change announcement — all-hands, email, one-on-ones, team meeting, written FAQ, informal check-in — before you send the first one. Walztoni's rule is that when you are tired of saying it, people are starting to get it.
- Map every person on your team on can-do and will-do against the role you actually need filled, then act on the single biggest gap this month: move them, add a resource, or reassign. Include at least one person you have never considered for anything outside their current lane.
