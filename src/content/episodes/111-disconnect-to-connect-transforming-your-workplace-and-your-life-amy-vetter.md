## Why this episode

Your firm says culture is a priority, and your partners say they do not have the time. Amy Vetter argues those are the same sentence, and that the second half is a story leaders tell themselves rather than a fact about their calendars. If you are trying to change how a firm works while everyone in it is busier and better paid than they have ever been, this is a specific account of where to start and who should be in the room.

## Guest

Amy Vetter is CEO of the B3 Method Institute, where B3 stands for business plus balance equals bliss, and her work spans workplace transformation, business development, client advisory services, and mindfulness training. She is a CPA, a yoga instructor, and a former yoga studio owner; she came to the practice at 32, when illness during a pregnancy left yoga as the only exercise her doctor would permit. She wrote Business, Balance & Bliss and Disconnect to Connect, the second of which she took notes toward for ten to twelve years before starting. Find her on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/amyvettercpa/).

## Key ideas

**"I don't have time for my people" is leadership self-deception, not a scheduling fact.** Vetter's account of the loop is precise: your first thought is that you should be spending an hour a day on your people, and then your mind supplies where would I find it and they never appreciate it anyway. The honest version is that you would have to hand off work to make the room.

**Seniority should invert your time allocation, and firms select against that.** As people rise through the ranks, more of their time should go to people rather than less. Yet managers are still chosen on technical skill, billable hours, and what they bring in, not on development, retention, or whether anyone is happy.

**The impossible forty-hour week is an inherited belief, not a feature of the profession.** Vetter traces it to leaders who suffered through it themselves and now expect the next person to buy in the same way. She files "this generation doesn't want to work as hard" under the same heading, and adds that the hardest condition for change is prosperity: everyone has made more money than they ever have, while the partner pipeline empties out underneath them.

**Partners should not write the work-life policy by themselves.** Vetter builds cross-level task forces to draft the standard operating procedures, because the closed-room version fails and then the failure gets attributed to ungrateful staff. Nobody in that story ever asked the staff what would actually make the place worth staying at.

**You do not own an idea, so stop defending it.** Vetter's position is that an idea that you do not act on will find someone who will, which is why "that was my idea" is usually wrong. Stay attached to the outcome and let other people expand the idea. She is candid that the detachment took her years, and that her team asks her not to have ideas on vacation.

**Hiding your own time off quietly forbids everyone else's.** Staff conclude that the partners work constantly, so Vetter blocks the hour on a visible calendar and says why. If you took Friday to golf and worked Saturday to cover it, both halves should be visible, and the second follower matters more than the first move.

## Try this

- Move your recurring meetings from 30 and 60 minutes to 25 and 50. Meetings expand to fill whatever you give them, so the gap costs you no information and buys two minutes to reset before the next conversation.
- Block one hour on a calendar your team can see, label it honestly, and tell them why it is there. If you take Friday afternoon and make it up on Saturday, let both halves be visible.
- Take the one work-life policy your partner group has been drafting privately and hand it to a task force with people from every level of the firm. Then find your second follower before the next partner meeting, not during it.
