## Why this episode

You do good technical work, and someone has told you, without much detail, that you also need to bring in business. Alex Egan came to consulting through the military, road patrol, homicide investigation, and five years at FINRA, and he argues that a client conversation runs on the same mechanics as an interrogation: build rapport, make the person comfortable, hold their attention long enough to get somewhere real. If you dislike selling and assume that rules you out, this episode makes the opposite case.

## Guest

Alex Egan is a director in the regulatory compliance practice at Kaufman Rossin, a full-service public accounting and consulting firm, where his team advises broker-dealers, registered investment advisors, banks, money services businesses, and fintech firms on compliance risk. He reached that job through the US military, road patrol and then detective work in Palatka, Florida, an investigator role at the Florida Department of Financial Services assigned to the FBI's South Florida Fraud Task Force, and five years at FINRA. He also pursued standup comedy on the side, which shows up in how he thinks about presenting. Find him on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/alex-egan-85a61b56/).

## Key ideas

**You are not pigeonholed by the industry you started in.** Egan's own path crosses four unrelated fields, and his read is that people narrow their search to roles that match their history 100 percent when the transferable skills are broader than that: research, compressing it into a short and usable answer, and building relationships with peers and bosses. The rest is learned on the job.

**Interrogation and client work are the same skill wearing different clothes.** As a detective, Egan says the best evidence usually came from the interview rather than from fingerprints on a weapon. Sit down, build rapport, make the person comfortable, hold the conversation long enough to reach something true. He is careful to note that he is describing the communication method, not the intent.

**In a small or mid-sized firm, technical excellence alone makes advancement a struggle.** Egan's test is to look left, right, up, and down in your organization, pick the people whose careers you want, and ask how they got there. He allows for exceptions, but says the brand of a mid-sized firm will not sell for you the way a Big Four name does.

**Some of the best business developers are introverts, which is the proof that this is coachable.** Egan was astonished to find that his own BD mentors did not draw energy from groups. He pairs that with a point people miss: prospects dislike salespeople too, so the job is not learning to wine and dine, it is communicating your value cleanly enough that someone understands why they should hire you.

**Sell in pairs, because talking up a colleague delivers your value without the bragging problem.** Egan finds it easier to sell other people than himself, and adds a second reason: buyers differ. The detail-first prospect and the relationship-first prospect want different people across the table, and clients will follow up with whichever of you they clicked with. He then turns it on firm leaders: if compensation makes origination individual, partners will not work together no matter how much you encourage it.

**Never memorize a pitch or a presentation.** Prepare the key points and not the wording, then open by asking someone in the room a question that pulls them into the first topic. Egan's warning is that the instinct to rehearse is exactly the trap for diligent, detail-oriented professionals, because everyone in the room can read a script.

## Try this

- Name three people in your firm whose careers you would take in ten years, and ask each of them this month how their work actually comes in. Egan's prediction is that business development appears in every answer.
- Bring one colleague to your next prospect meeting, ideally someone whose personality is unlike yours, and agree beforehand that whoever the prospect clicks with owns the follow-up.
- For your next presentation, write the key points on a single page and delete every full sentence. Then plan one opening question to someone in the room that leads into your first topic.
