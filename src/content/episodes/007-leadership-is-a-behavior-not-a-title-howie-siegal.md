## Why this episode

You were promoted because you were the best doer on the team, and now the work belongs to other people. This one is for the professional three to seven years in who is quietly finishing everyone's deliverables at 11pm and calling it leadership. Howie Siegal made exactly that mistake for years, and he can tell you what it cost him in people.

## Guest

Howie Siegal is an audit partner with RSM, with a sixteen-year career spent entirely in public accounting. He built a new RSM office in Wisconsin as a young manager, then moved to Detroit. He credits a firm leadership development program he went through a few years before making partner with changing how he leads — specifically the idea of the multiplier and its opposite. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/howardsiegal/).

## Key ideas

**Leading by example and getting the work done is not leadership.** Siegal believed for years that if he did the work well, people would follow. What actually multiplies a team is giving people accountability, challenging them and making them debate — not out-producing them.

**Rescuing someone who is stuck teaches them that someone else will finish it.** Siegal now refuses to hand over answers. He removes walls and barriers and trains, and his seniors have adjusted: they come into his office already knowing he will not tell them the answer, and want to talk it through anyway.

**Siegal's own turnover is the evidence, and he offers it against himself.** Almost nobody who worked for him in the first half of his Wisconsin tenure — the years he did the work himself — is still at the firm. Nearly everyone from the years after he changed approach is still there, and still calls him.

**The skills that get you to a point in your career are not the skills that advance you past it.** The doer-to-delegator shift usually hits somewhere between three and seven years in, and Siegal is blunt that plenty of people never make it. The tell is someone who can do the work but cannot build or keep a team.

**Train new hires to be business professionals, not auditors.** Siegal tells every incoming class that he is not training them to be auditors. You cannot undo twenty or thirty years of people expecting to own their own careers, and "you're the new guy, go do your job" does not attract, grow, or keep talent.

**Leadership requires no title, which is the whole argument.** A 22 or 23-year-old who listens, brings in different ideas and organizes without micromanaging is already leading. People gravitate to that person regardless of where they sit on the org chart.

## Try this

- For the next two weeks, require a proposed solution with every problem brought to you, and hold the line on the days when handing over the answer would be faster. Say out loud why you are doing it so it does not read as stonewalling.
- Put a facilitated 360 into this year's plan instead of asking people how you are doing. Ask explicitly for the view from below, and read the parts you disagree with twice before you respond to any of it.
- The next time a deadline forces you to take work back and finish it yourself, book twenty minutes with that person afterward and walk them through what they missed.
