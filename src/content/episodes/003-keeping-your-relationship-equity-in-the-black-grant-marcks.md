## Why this episode

You are measured on activity: teasers logged, calls made, cities visited. None of it tells you whether the relationships behind the numbers are worth anything. Grant Marcks runs origination for a private equity firm at the lower end of the middle market, and he argues the scoreboard is pointed at the wrong thing, because what compounds is a fast credible no and a habit of finding ways to get everyone around a deal paid. Use this one to check whether your funnel math is producing deals or just producing interactions.

## Guest

Grant Marcks is principal and head of business development for Atlantic Street Capital, where he has run origination across the firm's first four funds. He describes rebuilding the intermediary map as the firm moved from unknown quantity to known entity, and the conversation is unusually specific about how he does it: tiered CRM cadences, recorded channel preferences, and explicit targets for new firms added per quarter. He and host Alex Drost met from opposite sides of the table, when Drost was an investment banker showing him deals he mostly passed on. Find him at [linkedin.com/in/grant-marcks-722bb671](https://www.linkedin.com/in/grant-marcks-722bb671/).

## Key ideas

**Everyone ends a call offering to help, so the follow-through is the differentiator, and genuine interest is the differentiator beyond that.** Marcks treats paying it forward as a practice rather than a closing line, and is candid that keeping it up on a morning when your own life is loud is the hard part.

**A CRM does not make outreach less personal; it is what lets you get personal at scale.** Marcks records who wants a text, who wants an email, and who will happily spend twenty minutes on the phone shooting the bull. He caters channel to age and demographic rather than defaulting to his own preference, and calls the marriage of data and relationship table stakes going forward.

**Activity and productivity are different things, and a call quota manufactures the interactions it claims to measure.** He is indifferent between collecting ten deals to do five and collecting a thousand. If you tell yourself you need fifteen calls to get one deal, you will make fifteen calls; three or four curated ones might get you the same deal.

**Saying no quickly and for credible reasons is what makes your yes believable.** Marcks wants his firm to read as binary, so that interest means something. When he passes, he gives the reason and names other firms credibly active in that market, which is why bankers bring him the next one.

**Capital is a commoditized product, so the real job is finding ways to get your deal sources paid.** The fee pool inside a finite portfolio only stretches so far, and intermediaries are otherwise indifferent between him and the firm down the street. Pulling an accounting firm into the QOE and audit work is how he creates a reason to be chosen.

**Do not presume to know where your next deal will come from.** Five years earlier he would have bet on a specific set of investment banks; the deals actually came through a lender, an accounting firm, and a wealth advisor network. It is the single piece of advice he would hand his younger self.

## Try this

- Pull your last twenty passes. Count how many got a real reason and the names of two or three firms that would actually fit. For the ones that got a form decline, send the reason this week anyway.
- Add a channel-preference field to your CRM and fill it in for your top 25 contacts: text, email, or a twenty-minute call. Run next month's outreach off that field instead of off your own habit.
- Set a "go get" number for the quarter, a specific count of new firms and new contacts to add to your map, and put a recurring Friday morning block on the calendar to work it.
