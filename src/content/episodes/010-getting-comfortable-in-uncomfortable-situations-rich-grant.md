## Why this episode

You walk into rooms where nothing about your background matches the people in them, and you spend the first twenty minutes deciding whether to bluff. Rich Grant came into middle-market deal rooms from advertising, software and media, usually as the only Black man there, and he argues that position is the source of what he brings rather than a handicap on it. This one is for you if you are the outsider in the room, and also if you have never once had to be.

## Guest

Rich Grant is director of business development for GO, Growth Operators. He came to the middle market from advertising, software as a service and media, with no financial-adjacent experience at all, and had been in the world about two months when he attended his first major industry conference. He recorded this conversation in the summer of 2020 and talks directly about race in a way the rest of the show does not. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/gorichgrant/).

## Key ideas

**Admitting what you do not know is what earns the relationship.** At that first conference, the president of a large Chicago middle-market lender walked up to Grant's booth and Grant recognized neither the man nor the firm. He said so and let himself be taught, and it turned into a 25-minute conversation. Pretending to know is the biggest mistake available in that moment.

**When you cannot open on shared background, open on what you bring.** Grant calls it creating commonality through difference. He cannot assume a shared school, a shared neighborhood or a shared career path, so he shares who he is and the value he carries almost immediately, rather than fishing for overlap that may not exist.

**Hard work is not sufficient, and pretending otherwise is a comfortable inaccuracy.** Grant names people he and Drost both know who work very hard in these same rooms and do not see the same results. He puts racial diversity and diversity of thought in one frame: a room where everyone was schooled the same way is robbing itself of the perspectives that never made it in.

**"Agree to disagree" is a retreat to your corner, not a resolution.** Drost calls it one of the most unhealthy moves available and offers the marriage version of it, and Grant extends it to the country: if you will not talk about the flaws, you cannot fix them. His alternative is what he calls thinking independently together — bring your experience, hear mine, and go somewhere neither of us started.

**Racism has no other side. How to build a diverse firm does.** Grant draws the line explicitly. Difference of opinion should not be a deal breaker, but there is no two-sidedness on racism, including for people who merely stay adjacent to racist ideas. The means of getting to diversity of race, gender and thought are genuinely debatable, and he wants that argument had rather than avoided.

**You do not need authority to change a room.** Grant's ask is small and physical: step into a room where you are the one who cannot relate to everyone. If your community has no racial diversity to offer, leave the neighborhood, sit down and listen. Carrying that perspective back to your own circle compounds without anyone handing you a microphone.

## Try this

- Before your next conference, pick one person whose firm you could not describe if asked, and open by saying exactly that. Let them explain what they do before you say anything about what you do.
- List the last ten people you had a real conversation with at work. If every one of them came up the way you did, put one coffee on the calendar this month with someone whose route into the industry looks nothing like yours.
- The next time a hard conversation drifts toward "let's agree to disagree," ask one more question instead — why do you see it that way — and stay in it for another five minutes.
