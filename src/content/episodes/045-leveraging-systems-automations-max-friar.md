## Why this episode

If you run a transaction process, your capacity to serve the client is set by how much administrative work you are still doing by hand — and you probably don't experience it that way. Max Friar makes the harder version of the argument: manual NDA and follow-up work caps how broadly you can market a client's business, and a market you cannot show is a fiduciary duty you cannot discharge. This is the operations episode, with the numbers and the send times attached.

## Guest

Max Friar is Managing Partner at Calder Capital, a full-service M&A firm focused on lower middle-market businesses in the Midwest. He started in mergers and acquisitions at 25, worked at two firms before founding Calder in 2013, and has sold a business himself. Calder closed 23 transactions in 2019 and 40 in 2020. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/business-broker-grand-rapids-mi).

## Key ideas

**Broad confidential marketing is a fiduciary obligation, not a preference, and Friar treats it as the test of whether the job was done.** His math is roughly a hundred buyers to surface the ten willing to have a real conversation. Until the seller has met that many, neither of you can know you found the best fit — so the industry standard of a twelve-month exclusive, a large retainer and one buyer by month six reads to him as a failure of the role.

**The chronic complaint that brokers don't respond is a capacity failure, not a service attitude.** Ten inquiries are enough to bury someone sending NDAs, chasing questionnaires and maintaining a spreadsheet by hand. Friar points out that some brokers now write the backlog into their own advertisements.

**Automating the front end costs nothing in personalization, because none of it was ever the personal part.** Signing an NDA, filling out a questionnaire, watermarking a CIM, logging who received what: Friar's position, which Alex Drost confirms from his own banking career, is that this work has no relationship content at all. Ninety percent of the buyers who inquire will do nothing, so human attention should start only after a buyer has self-selected through the automated steps.

**The real payoff is emotional bandwidth, not efficiency.** With the admin removed, a Calder advisor can carry four to five sell-side projects at once and still have the capacity to sit with a client through what Friar calls the separation and the trauma of selling. An advisor mentally drafting NDAs during that conversation cannot do that work.

**For 1-to-15-million-dollar family businesses, cultural fit and legacy often outweigh price and terms.** Employees who have been there thirty years and no natural successor change what the seller is optimizing for. Friar describes the recognition as instinctual — something the owner feels after meeting a buyer — which is precisely why the market has to be shown broadly enough for them to have the feeling at all.

**The business owners who never automate are the ones who show up wanting to sell, and their companies are worth less for it.** Friar's word for it is blunt: retirement is often code for exhausted, and it arrives in someone's forties or fifties. The company is smaller, less profitable, and harder to replace the owner in, because the owner never stepped out of any step of the process.

## Try this

- Move your outbound sends to Tuesday through Friday, 11am to 1pm, kill the Saturday send, and put the company name in the subject line instead of the individual's. Calder had two million of its own emails analyzed to get those three findings and saw a difference two weeks after implementing them.
- Take Alex Drost's call to action: find 30 minutes this week to write down three tasks you enjoy in your day-to-day work and three you wish you didn't have to deal with. Tasks, not people. The second list is your automation backlog.
- Book two or three days away from your office, alone, with the explicit purpose of reflecting rather than resting. Friar's observation is that the owners who tell him they haven't taken a vacation in five years are the burnout cases who end up on his sell-side list.
