## Why this episode

You know you are supposed to be investing in your own development, and another year has gone by in which the only teacher was whatever your last engagement happened to demonstrate. Elliott Holland works in buy-side due diligence, where things go wrong publicly and expensively, and he treats each of those as a classroom he has already paid for and refuses to leave early. Listen if your growth is currently accidental and you want a way to make it deliberate without adding a course to your calendar.

## Guest

Elliott Holland is a managing director with Guardian Due Diligence, where he and his team help family offices execute direct private equity deals. He is an engineer by training who worked unpaid at a private equity firm during his first and second years at Harvard Business School, walking in every day not knowing what he was doing and asking for help until he did. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/elliottholland/).

## Key ideas

**You are paid to solve problems, and the skills that solve them change under you.** Holland's framing is relative rather than absolute: any stretch you spend not investing in yourself, you are moving backwards against the people who are. His example is the classical investor who now has to learn cryptocurrency at sixty.

**Every engagement should return money or a lesson, and you can design for both.** He reaches for design of experiment from his engineering training. If the work is set up so that it cannot produce either outcome, you have sub-optimized before you started.

**When a deal turns, the expensive part is already spent, so stay for the lesson.** Sixty days into a ninety-day window you find the seller's fib or the broker you can no longer trust. Walking away is the obvious move; Holland's addition is to call the quality-of-earnings provider and ask where the problem was hidden and what would have surfaced it sooner. He once finished a two-day trip he should have left after half a day, specifically to get the lesson.

**The top ten percent of your professional frustration marks where your next advantage is.** When Holland is that angry, it means none of his safeguards worked — which makes it either his largest growth opportunity or a competitive advantage waiting to be learned. He applies it to something as mundane as failing to find a decent CRM.

**Smart, prepared people rarely make mistakes; you are missing their constraints.** He disagrees on face with the habit of calling an accomplished person stupid when they do something you did not expect. The productive move is to whiteboard the motives, incentives and constraints you could not see, then work out which one you missed.

**Intuition is built from reps, and part of maturing is deciding which feedback to override.** After something blows up, the people you trust will hand you conflicting readings of what you did wrong. Holland's test is whether the comment hurts or matters, on the grounds that you know the situation better than the person diagnosing it.

## Try this

- Block thirty minutes this week on your most recent finished engagement and answer three questions in writing: what would you continue, what would you stop, what would you start. Keep the answers where you will see them before the next one starts.
- Call the advisor or teammate from your last deal that went sideways and ask one thing — where was the problem hidden, and what would have surfaced it before you were sixty days in?
- Ask someone twenty or thirty years ahead of you in your field for a coffee this month, ask what they would do differently and what mistakes they made, and then say nothing else.
