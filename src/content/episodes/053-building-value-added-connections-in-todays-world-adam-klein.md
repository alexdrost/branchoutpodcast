## Why this episode

You are back to being asked for coffee, and you are not sure which invitations to take. This conversation gives you a rule for the first meeting, a rule for travel, and a reason to stop treating your own preference about video as a principle. It is also the episode where Alex states his hardest tactical position on the show: he does not expect to take an introductory meeting in person again.

## Guest

Adam Klein is Chief Growth Officer with Bennett Thrasher, a full-service CPA and advisory firm based in Atlanta. He describes himself as a career business development and sales person, working inside an industry where, as he puts it, "sell" is treated as a four-letter word. Find him on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/adamkleinatlanta/).

## Key ideas

**Video is a permanent addition to the toolkit, not a substitute waiting to be discarded.** Klein lines it up with the letter, the phone, email, chat, and text: each one arrived, leveled out, and stayed. Zoom fatigue will settle; the tool will not go away.

**People now disagree about what counts as a face-to-face meeting, and that disagreement is yours to manage.** Some counterparties mean a desk between you. Others will say with equal confidence that seeing your face on video is face to face. Klein's position is that a hard line either way filters your network down to the people who already share your preference.

**Alex's rule: no in-person first meetings.** A coffee costs two hours by the time you drive, park, sit, and drive back, for something a fifteen- to thirty-minute call accomplishes. Do the intro on video, and spend the meal on the second meeting, once you know there is common ground. Klein agrees for intros and reserves in-person for closing and higher-stakes conversations.

**Virtual makes previously unreachable people reachable.** The reason is not your convenience but theirs: a thirty-minute call from their desk does not cost them the two hours a coffee costs. Klein would rather have thirty minutes next week than an hour in person three months out.

**A networking event is for brand, reputation, and network, not pipeline.** Klein's anchoring story is a junior colleague who called a holiday chamber event a waste of time because he did not sell anything. The expectation was the failure, not the event.

**Professional services cannot be compared apples to apples, so the decision reduces to trust.** Alex's example is a quality of earnings report: five firms produce five sets of numbers, five formats, five sets of adjustments. Klein's version is blunter — clients pick the more expensive provider they like better, and stay with partners who made mistakes.

## Try this

- Take your next three introductions as thirty-minute video calls, and hold the coffee or the meal for a second meeting with the ones where you found common ground.
- Before your next business development trip, run thirty-minute video calls with everyone on the list, then book meals with only the three or four you clicked with.
- Write down three ways you can be helpful to people already in your network this week — an introduction, a resource, a call with no agenda — and do them with zero expectation of return.
