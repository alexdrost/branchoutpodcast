## Why this episode

You are early in your career, you go to the events, you hand over the resume, and nothing happens — because the senior person you handed it to is holding a dozen more and can help almost none of them. Paul Gray's answer is to stop asking and start owning something worth giving: a podcast, a blog, a newsletter, any platform a senior person wants access to. This is the tactical version of "add value first," including who to approach in the room and who to skip.

## Guest

Paul Gray is co-founder and managing partner of Ironhold Capital, chairman of Leaders in Business and Investing, and president of the Capital Allocators Round Table. He built a show that runs 20,000 to 25,000 viewers an episode and used it to sit across from Anthony Scaramucci, Joel Greenblatt, Bruce Greenwald and Jay Abraham while raising a hedge fund. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/pauljohngray/) and [Twitter](https://twitter.com/realpaulgray).

## Key ideas

**The resume is the wrong instrument, because the senior person you hand it to is already holding dozens and can help almost none of them.** Gray's reframe is that standing out requires arriving with something to give rather than something to ask. If you want into real estate, know two buildings a developer could make money on. If you want into funds, know two people a manager could call about capital.

**A media platform is a giveable asset because exposure raises the guest's own market value.** Gray is direct about the mechanics: appearing in front of an audience makes someone more hireable or more promotable, which is why a student with a school newspaper can get an interview with a notable fund. The platform, not the request, is what makes the outreach work.

**Never network with people your own age.** His reasoning is two-part and unsentimental — same-level contacts have no authority inside their own firms, and they are competing with you for the same positions. Find the gray hair. This is the sharpest contrarian claim in the episode and it is worth arguing with.

**In-person events are the trickiest venue to connect, and they only work with advance research.** Everyone in the room is selling. Gray's method is to get the attendee list beforehand, decide who you want, and then target the senior people who were not on stage that night — because the speaker has a line and the equally important attendees standing next to them do not.

**"Work hard" is useless advice, and Gray says so plainly.** His analogy: telling someone all they need to fly a jet is gasoline. Hard work is fuel, not mechanism. What transfers is who you surround yourself with, which idols in your field you study exhaustively, how you allocate and protect your hours, and a willingness to launch before the plan is finished.

**A following is portable career insurance, which makes profile-building a defensive move as well as an offensive one.** Gray's argument is that the audience and the relationships belong to you rather than your employer. If a recession costs you the job, both travel with you — which is why he tells young professionals to build the profile now, not at the point they need it.

## Try this

- Before your next event, get the attendee list and pick three senior names who are not speaking. Write one specific thing you could offer each of them — an introduction, a piece of research, a slot on whatever you publish.
- Start the smallest possible platform this month: a LinkedIn interview series, a newsletter, a recorded conversation. Then use it to request one interview with someone you would never cold-email, and say in the request what the exposure does for them.
- If someone has already given you their platform, go finish the job — edit it properly, post it, and promote it more than once. Gray's complaint is about hosts who let an appearance disappear after a single share, and it is the fastest way to burn the relationship you just built.
