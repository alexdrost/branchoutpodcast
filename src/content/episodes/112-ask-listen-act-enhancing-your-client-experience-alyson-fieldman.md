## Why this episode

You do good work, and so does the firm down the street, so promising a prospect accuracy and technical depth buys you nothing. Alyson Fieldman's argument is that for a firm without a narrow specialization, the remaining differentiator is how clients feel about working with you — and that this is a program you run rather than a project you finish. It is also the last episode of Branch Out, which makes it a fitting close: after more than a hundred conversations about connection as the engine of a professional career, the last one is about building the machinery to keep asking clients what they actually think.

## Key ideas

**Client service is what the provider does; client experience is how the client feels about it.** Fieldman defines experience as the sum of every touch point — how the phone is answered, how the invoice reads, how often anyone visits — and how those land emotionally. Alex Drost uses the wrong term mid-episode and she corrects it on the spot, which is a fair measure of how easily the two get conflated.

**Ask, listen, act — and act is the step firms skip.** Asking scales from a lunch check-in to an annual survey to structured feedback interviews. Collecting the answers and doing nothing is worse than never asking, because it tells the client exactly how much their view mattered.

**One in seven clients has an unvoiced complaint, so partner confidence is not evidence.** The first obstacle Fieldman meets is hubris — my clients are happy, you do not need to talk to them — usually paired with possessiveness about whose clients they are. The unvoiced complaints are where the value is, and they only surface if someone asks.

**The second obstacle is nobody's idea of strategy: you probably cannot produce a clean client list.** Relationship owners live in the CRM, billing contacts live in time and billing, and nothing reconciles the two. Getting to one reviewed list, with partner sign-off, can take months — and that work is the actual start date of most programs.

**A recovered failure can beat a relationship where nothing went wrong.** Responding fast and visibly to a breakdown often produces a stronger client experience than an untroubled engagement, which reframes a surfaced complaint as an asset. Fieldman also accepts the other outcome: if you can never deliver what a client needs, referring them elsewhere is the right answer.

**It is a program, not a project, and NPS alone will not carry it.** A one-off annual survey yields insight without compounding outcomes. Her design notes: keep to seven or eight questions, ask about value rather than whether your price is fair, include open-ended questions and one about the client's goals for the year, and have the interview run by someone who understands the business but is not on the account.

## Try this

- Map the client journey yourself first — prospect, pitch, engagement letter, onboarding, delivery, the quiet months, renewal — then call three clients and ask what each of those stages felt like from their side. The gap between the two maps is your work list.
- Put a seven-question survey in front of one service line this quarter. Ask about value rather than whether your pricing is fair, ask what you could do differently, and ask what their top three goals are for the coming year.
- Before the next portal, app or onboarding process goes live, put clients in the room: a roundtable with current users, or a 30-day trial with a handful of preferred clients who agree to report back.
