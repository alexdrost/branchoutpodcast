## Why this episode

You are weighing whether to leave a salaried professional role for your own thing, and everyone you ask talks about the first year. Isatou Smith gives you year four, because that is when her income finally passed what she had been earning before she left, and year three is when she was still seriously considering going back. If you want the financial arc of going independent stated plainly, and an honest account of what keeps people in it, start here.

## Guest

Isatou Smith is managing director and cofounder of EisnerAmper Government Services Ltd. in Grand Cayman, with roughly 20 years in financial services. She began at the Cayman regulator working in investment funds, spent seven years at a large Cayman law firm doing directorships, then went out on her own before partnering with EisnerAmper in 2018. Her firm sits as independent directors on fund boards and takes on the anti-money-laundering, compliance, and know-your-client work that fund managers cannot staff in house. Find her on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/isatou-smith-785a2947/).

## Key ideas

**Going independent pays in autonomy long before it pays in money.** Smith set aside enough salary for a year or two and expected to be past her old income by year three. Year three she was still struggling. Year four was when she passed it, and what had carried her there was control over her own day rather than the earnings.

**The leap is the easy part; the endurance afterward is what ends most attempts.** By the time you resign you have already argued yourself into it, and Alex Drost describes that moment as being hyped up and ready. The third, fourth, and fifth missed paycheck are where the real decision gets made.

**If income is what drives you, Smith's advice is to stay.** She watched friends try the same move and come back inside a year, and her read is that they wanted immediate success: the salary they had left, plus more of their own time, starting now. That is not what the first years hand you.

**The visible version of the life hides the unpaid part.** People saw her at a coffee shop or picking up her kids. What they did not see was the late nights on LinkedIn, the trips to New York paid for out of her own pocket, and steady rejection for years before a former client called and asked whether she was still in the industry.

**Comparing yourself to peers is the thing that most often ends the attempt.** While you are rebuilding, the people who stayed are buying cars and houses on schedule. Smith's counter is to go back to the goals you wrote at the start — hers included family time and travel, not only a number — and measure against that document rather than against the people around you.

**Nobody will impose structure on you, so the discipline has to be manufactured.** Her version is not rigor for its own sake. If your agency means starting the day at 10, start at 10 every day and hold it, because the only person who will notice you did not show up is you, and you will notice it years late.

## Try this

- Write down this week what you want out of the move that is not money: time with family, travel, control of your calendar. Date it and file it somewhere you will find it in three years, because you will need something to measure against other than your peers' cars.
- Pick your start time and hold it for the next two weeks. Ten o'clock is a legitimate answer if choosing it is the point; drifting is not.
- Put one recurring non-work hour on the calendar and defend it like a client meeting. Smith names a workout, a long walk, or yoga. The requirement is that it repeats, not what it is.
