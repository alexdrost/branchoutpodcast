## Why this episode

Your firm keeps running the training that worked on you at twenty-two and cannot understand why partners will not do it. Bruce Ditman spent about eleven and a half years as Chief Marketing Officer at Marcum before going independent, and his diagnosis is that firms confuse two different things — indoctrination, which only works on people who are frightened and hungry for the right answer, and education, which is an exchange of value with an adult. If you are responsible for growing people or for getting anybody to sell, this is a conversation about why the standard playbook fails and what replaces it.

## Guest

Bruce Ditman is the founder of Chief Seconds, a marketing and growth consultancy built on the idea that useful is beautiful. He studied languages, not marketing, and came up through a talent agency trainee program and television advertising sales at CBS before an accounting firm, UHY, hired him to run marketing. He became CMO at Marcum LLP three months after Marcum acquired UHY's New England office, and held the role for roughly eleven and a half years. ## Key ideas

**Indoctrination works on the young because they are terrified, and it cannot work on a partner.** Ditman is not using the word loosely — he calls it a powerful tool, and says the leaders who cannot understand why nobody wants the two-week program have forgotten that it is how they got their own training. At twenty-two, every screw-up felt like a threat to the rest of your life, which is exactly the state in which being told the right way to do things sticks. A forty-five-year-old with equity has perspective and no reason to comply.

**Adults acquire skills through an exchange of value, so the onus is on the educator to make the business case.** He frames the difference as asymmetry: indoctrination is one-directional and easy to land because the audience is desperate. Education is not. His test for the culture is blunt — anyone in your organization with no interest in acquiring skills belongs on the HR watch list, because that incuriosity will show up in client service.

**Putting your rainmakers in front of the firm is the worst possible teaching choice.** Two reasons, and he means both. Naturals cannot articulate the mechanics that take someone from zero to twenty, because they never had to think about them. And they are the most intimidating person in the room, so the audience checks out. He borrows his firm's name from the boxing corner: the best coaches were journeyman fighters, never contenders, because they had to learn everything.

**Teach fundamentals so people are not limited by their talent.** The goal is not more superstars. It is "Oh, Susanna" on the ukulele — enough of a foundation that nobody is held back for the sole reason that they were never taught. Ditman's arithmetic: a ten percent lift in top line is achievable through training people who currently do zero business development, which might mean going as far as talking about where they work at Thanksgiving.

**Manufacturing crisis is a fallacy, and governing education with a stick has no exit.** Nobody performs better when they are afraid, and he says generating crises to force a response is a common management pattern that produces lockup instead of results. On training specifically, his image is horse racing: once you whip a horse you always have to whip a horse, so a compliance frame yields "I passed" where you wanted "I earned a degree." He and Drost both put annual CPE credits in this category — a fifty-minute session with three multiple-choice questions is not how strategic thinking gets taught.

**There is no such thing as a great boss with a terrible team, and the partnership model keeps producing both.** Hiring, firing and profitability all belong to the leader, so a complaint about the team is a self-diagnosis. Ditman's one-sentence job description for a manager is to give credit and take blame; when a team knows their success is your success, you do not need to scare them. He attributes the prevalence of the complaint to structure: partnerships are not enterprises, management training for new partners is scant, and operational functions get treated as cost centers.

## Try this

- Write a target list on a pad next to your phone — actual names, numbers and email addresses, not a demographic. Ditman's own list was largely fiction on Monday and half-completed by Friday, because the writing removed the barrier to starting.
- Before your next prospect meeting, spend thirty minutes getting conversationally fluent in their sector, then plan to ask one follow-up you could only ask if you had listened. "Why do you think that is?" is his default.
- If you are trying to keep someone, ask them what they actually want instead of offering what you assume they should want. Then change one visible behavior of your own — leaving the office at a normal hour is his example — rather than announcing a policy.
