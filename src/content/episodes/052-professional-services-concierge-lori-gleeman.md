## Why this episode

You sell professional services into private equity, or you buy them, and either way the selection process is somebody asking a friend. Lori Gleeman built a business in that gap, and she is unusually direct about how it works — who gets into her network, who she turns down, and who pays her. If you are wondering why your firm keeps losing work you were qualified for, her answer is that the decision was made before anyone read your credentials.

## Guest

Lori Gleeman is Founder and CEO of Soul Equity Solutions, a professional services concierge for middle market private equity firms, independent sponsors and family offices. She spent her earlier career as an executive search recruiter placing leaders into private equity portfolio companies, which is where she noticed the gap her current business fills. She describes herself as doing B2B recruiting: introducing one firm to another rather than a person to a firm. Find her on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/lorigleeman/).

## Key ideas

**Private equity firms apply serious diligence to executive hires and almost none to the providers they hire.** Gleeman watched firms spend enormous time and energy recruiting portfolio-company executives, then choose their quality of earnings, IT or consulting provider from whoever a friend in another fund happened to mention. The mismatch is the whole premise of her business.

**A referral network is only worth anything if it is curated by exclusion.** Every provider in Gleeman's network arrived recommended by an existing private equity client, and then cleared two additional references. She asks the question deliberately at the end of client conversations: who are the very best providers you work with. A firm that has done one transaction and wants in gets told no, because she is not there to launch anyone's private equity practice.

**When the deliverable is near-commoditized, the buying decision collapses into trust in the referrer.** Three quality of earnings reports should come out roughly the same, and the buyer cannot tell in advance which firm is better. So the transaction runs on whether they trust the person pointing. Gleeman argues her referral adds a second layer of accountability on top of the client relationship, because providers do not want to lose their place in the network.

**The value that builds the relationship is the value she was not asked for and does not sell.** She opens client calls with what they are working on and which industries they are looking at, then raises what they have not named — ESG, cyber security, candidate assessments, executive coaching. One client had run eleven years with zero cyber security and had simply never had it put in front of them.

**Savings-share pricing usually costs the buyer more, and efficiency work beats the reputation private equity has earned.** Operating consultants who take a percentage of the savings they create feel risk-free, and Gleeman's read is that clients typically pay more that way than they would with a fee upfront. She is equally direct that creating savings by tightening operations rather than firing a third of the staff is both better business and the answer to the reputation the industry carries.

## Try this

- End your next three client conversations by asking which providers they rate most highly and why. Do not pass any of those names along until you have checked two references yourself.
- Come to your next client call with one need they have not named and that you do not sell — cyber, ESG, candidate assessment, coaching. Raising it costs you nothing and is the specific behavior Gleeman credits for her best relationships.
- If you are about to sign an operating consultant on a percentage of savings, price the same scope as a flat fee first and compare. Gleeman's experience is that the contingent version ends up costing more.
