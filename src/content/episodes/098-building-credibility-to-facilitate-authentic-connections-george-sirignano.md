## Why this episode

You bought the system, built the shared infrastructure, and collaboration still is not happening. George Sirignano runs a broker-dealer platform where roughly 100 independent capital raisers are meant to refer work to each other, and after a year and a half of setup he found it still took three or four full-time people talking to them by hand. If you are trying to get producers who do not report to you — affiliates, partners, other service lines — to share what they know, his answer is that the mechanism is a trusted person, not a database.

## Guest

George Sirignano is managing partner of Old City Investment Partners and, as a lawyer by background, acts as counsel for the firm. Old City raises capital for fund managers and private companies, runs about 15 people between New York and London, and meets roughly 500 fund managers a year to select five or six. Before this he worked at a large New York corporate firm, spent a year with a UN agency in Africa, ran Sotheby's Latin America operations, and worked at Guggenheim Partners. ## Key ideas

**"If we build it, they will come" is the wrong theory of collaboration.** Old City went through what Sirignano calls a year and a half of anguish standing up its own broker dealer, on the assumption that the infrastructure would make cross-referral happen by itself. It did not. What makes it happen is a staff of three or four people doing inside sales to their own affiliates, every day.

**A producer's call notes are their intellectual property, and the best producers guard them hardest.** Old City deliberately does not mandate a central CRM, because Sirignano found the most tenured and productive affiliates were the least likely to contribute. The rule he lands on: centralize what people are comfortable volunteering — who they know, what strategies those investors want — and mediate the rest through conversation.

**The platform's actual product is being trusted with information nobody would share peer-to-peer.** Because Old City sits in the middle and is believed to be discreet, an affiliate will say "I have an investor, don't talk to anybody until you tell me what you think." Sirignano frames that as solving a collective action problem: left alone, the same people would not trade information at all.

**An internal connector stops being independent the moment the money runs through someone they report to.** Sirignano says accounting and law firms need the same trusted, independent figure in-house. Alex Drost adds the failure mode — that person usually reports to a partner who controls a group and its compensation — and Sirignano agrees the perception of that dependency is what breaks it.

**Perceived conflicts have to be attacked head-on, even when there is no real conflict.** The worry about protesting too much is, in his experience, the more expensive mistake. He calls the person, tells them the situation, and names exactly what will not be divulged. Credibility on conflicts is what he thinks gives an organization like his any longevity.

**In-person time is not a warmer version of a call; it is where the unplanned opening appears.** One Old City team member spent years having what looked like an absurd number of coffees and is now one of their most productive investor-facing people. A hard-to-reach family office opened because he listened to someone's problem — a spouse looking for work — and did something about it.

## Try this

- Name your five most valuable internal relationships and put a recurring monthly touch on each one. Sirignano finds the collaboration quietly stops producing below that cadence, even with people he considers close.
- The next time a colleague could reasonably suspect a conflict, call them before it becomes a question. Say what the situation is and name exactly what you will not pass along.
- Book one distraction-free hour in person this month — a coffee, a 5:00 PM beer — with nothing to pitch, and leave the phone alone. Ask what is going on in their life and act on one thing you hear.
