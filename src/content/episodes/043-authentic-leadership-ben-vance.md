## Why this episode

You are responsible for work that has to be perfect and for people who cannot be. Ben Vance names the specific way those two standards collide: a deliverable goes through ten or fifteen iterations before a client sees it, while a conversation with a team member happens once, unedited, and often when you are least equipped for it. If your instinct under a deadline is to take the work back and fix it yourself, this episode is about what that instinct is costing the people you are supposed to be developing.

## Guest

Ben Vance is Director of Business Valuation and Transaction Advisory with Postlethwaite & Netterville. He is a CPA who spent his career in transaction work and describes reaching the point where the technical fundamentals stop being the thing he is learning. He tests most of his leadership ideas against coaching his eight-year-old son in the batting cage, and says repeatedly through the episode that he is still learning this. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ben-vance-cpa-abv-77375125).

## Key ideas

**Management is the fifteen-draft deliverable; leadership is the single take.** Vance's framing is the spine of the episode. A client product gets drafted, edited, polished and formatted before anyone outside sees it, so the standard is perfection — but an interaction with a person happens once, which means a leader has to be practiced at being wrong in front of people in a way a manager never has to be.

**Vance names his own default as good for deadlines and bad for development.** His natural inclination is to stop, tweak and change work until it is the way he wants it. He is direct that this behavior serves the delivery date and hurts long-term growth, and that which one wins depends on his mental state and how many projects are stacked on him that week.

**Where you let someone fail is a stakes question, not a philosophy question.** Nobody should ship the client deliverable unreviewed. But handing a junior the target output — here is the model I am trying to build, go build it — without the six-step recipe is the version of failure worth paying for, even if you have to redo the work.

**Growth runs on alternating stress and recovery, and part of the job is forcing recovery on people whose full load you cannot see.** Vance points to the team member who is also caring for a relative and studying for an exam, and who has no gap to recover in. Noticing that in someone else is also, he says, the reminder that he needs the same thing.

**The 11pm email cancels the recovery you just prescribed, whatever it says.** Vance catches himself telling people to close the laptop and then sending a message at night that says don't worry about this until morning. Alex Drost's addition is blunt: the team knows what it means when something lands in their inbox on a Saturday, regardless of the words in it.

**Finishing projects is a motivator with diminishing returns, so a technical career eventually has to be re-anchored.** Vance says the satisfaction of delivering shrinks with each delivery, and that what replaced it for him is asking what he learned, what he taught and who he connected with on a project. Acting like someone you are not spends the same finite energy, and the bill usually arrives at home, where the family gets the empty tank.

## Try this

- Pick one intermediate work product this month — a model, a schedule, an analysis that is not going to the client unreviewed — and hand it over as an outcome rather than a recipe. Say what you are trying to build, not the steps, and put the rework time in your own schedule up front so you are not tempted to take it back.
- Open your sent folder and count the messages you sent your team after 8pm or on a weekend in the last two weeks. If that number is not zero and you have also told them to disconnect, either stop sending or start scheduling delivery for the morning.
- At the end of your next project, write three lines before you start the next one: what you learned, what you taught, and who you connected with. Vance uses this to replace the fading satisfaction of the checked box.
