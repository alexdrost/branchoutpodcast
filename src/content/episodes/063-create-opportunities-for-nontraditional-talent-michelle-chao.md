## Why this episode

You want a wider talent pool and you keep going back to the same recruiters and the same schools. Michelle Chao started at Bertram Capital as an executive assistant, was told at her interview that the role had no growth path, and is now the firm's head of investor relations fourteen years later. Her account is useful because it names the two specific things that made the move possible, and both are things a manager controls.

## Guest

Michelle Chao is Head of Investor Relations and Senior Vice President of Originations at Bertram Capital, a private equity firm focused on lower middle market companies. She joined when the firm was less than a year old, as an executive assistant supporting business development, and taught herself the technical side through Wall Street Prep and sit-downs with each partner. Her graduate degree is a Masters in Child Education. Find her at [linkedin.com/in/michelle-chao-8480024](https://www.linkedin.com/in/michelle-chao-8480024/).

## Key ideas

**A junior person will not tell you they want a different job unless you ask them directly.** Chao asks everyone entering Bertram what they want to do and what interests them, and keeps asking as they progress. Her reasoning is that the more junior someone is, the more hesitant they are to raise it, so the burden of starting the conversation sits with the manager.

**Exposure is the constraint, not ability.** Chao did not know private equity existed when she started; she knew investment banking and venture capital. Bertram now runs an internship pipeline with a local nonprofit, Peninsula Bridge, and has each department present what it does at an all-hands, so people can see that a private equity firm also contains HR, accounting and administration.

**Take the hiring risk at the junior level, where the missing attributes can be trained.** Chao concedes that going outside the norm for a senior hire is much harder for most firms. Her filter for juniors is work ethic, intelligence and whether the person will stay; everything else, she argues, is teachable over time.

**An advocate supplies the belief the candidate does not yet have.** Chao did not put herself forward for the investor relations role. Her leadership told her she would be fantastic at it, acknowledged she had no IR background, and offered it anyway. Without that, she says, she would not have known the opportunity existed and would not have seen the potential in herself.

**Retention through motherhood was a managerial reaction, not a policy.** The first question she was asked was whether she wanted to stay, and only then how it would work. What followed was a negotiation with concrete terms: never more than three nights away in a week, make it up the following week, work from home Fridays.

**Chao rejects putting a deadline on diversity recruiting.** Bertram's approach is a standing intent — asking each other continuously whether they know women who might want into private equity — with no expectation of quick results. Her position is that expecting fast results is precisely what makes firms conclude it is impossible and stop.

## Try this

- In your next one-on-one with each person who reports to you, ask what they want to be doing in three years and which department they are curious about. Write the answers down and raise them again at the following review.
- Post your next open role publicly on LinkedIn instead of routing it only through a recruiter, and ask three people in your network this month whether they know a candidate from outside your usual pipeline.
- Pick one person on your team you think has more in them than their current seat allows, and tell them so this week, naming the specific role you can see them in.
