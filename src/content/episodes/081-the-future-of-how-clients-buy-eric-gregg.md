## Why this episode

Your firm grows on referrals and you have never seen a reason to care what the internet says about you. Eric Gregg runs the satisfaction and ratings data for professional service firms, and his numbers say two thirds of referred buyers Google you first, that buying groups now run five to eight people, and that risk aversion rises with group size. If you want to know what a prospect has already concluded before your first meeting, this is the episode with the figures.

## Guest

Eric Gregg is Founder and CEO of ClearlyRated, which measures client and employee satisfaction for professional service firms and publishes the resulting ratings and testimonials. Early in his career he worked with David Maister on the research behind *Practice What You Preach*, linking employee engagement to financial performance in service businesses, and he has stayed on that problem since. ClearlyRated runs an annual study of B2B buyers of professional services, now in its eighth year. Find him at [linkedin.com/in/ericgregg](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ericgregg/).

## Key ideas

**Ratings now rival referrals, and the referral itself gets checked.** Gregg's buyer study shows online ratings used as often as or more often than referrals, with two thirds of referred buyers searching the firm before acting. His framing of the younger cohort is not that they distrust you, it is that they crowdsource a second opinion as a matter of course.

**The information asymmetry that protected firms is gone.** Twenty years ago the accounting firm held the information the buyer wanted. Gregg cites Gartner and Forrester data putting B2B buyers roughly 80 percent of the way to a decision before they speak to anyone at the company, which means most of your pitch lands after the decision is substantially made.

**Bigger buying groups make you compete on risk, not just fit.** Gregg puts the typical group at five to eight people and cites Corporate Executive Board research showing risk aversion rises with group size. The person putting their name behind the recommendation needs to be able to defend it, so being the least risky option now matters alongside being the best one.

**Volume of proof beats a perfect score.** Gregg's studies find a 4.3 rating with 125 reviews outperforms a 4.9 with three. Buyers read a handful of five-star reviews as unproven, and the same logic explains why strangers now sleep in each other's homes: institutional trust is at record lows while peer trust is at record highs.

**Firms market capability when buyers are asking about consistency.** Gregg's version of the buyer's question is blunt: I have it down to three firms that could do it, who is most likely to actually do it? Capability content still matters, but it answers a question the buyer has already resolved.

**Proactivity is extra credit, and utilization metrics are why nobody does it.** Responsiveness, quality and hitting deadlines get you a score out of 100. Telling a client the question they should be asking is how you exceed it, or recover from a project that went imperfectly. Gregg blames the incentive rather than the individual: a couple of unbilled hours a week reaching out proactively shows up nowhere in the KPIs.

## Try this

- Search your own firm the way a referred buyer would, from a logged-out browser, and write down what the first screen actually shows. If there is no third-party evidence of quality anywhere on it, that is the gap Gregg's data describes.
- Block two hours on one afternoon a week for unbilled client outreach, and use the data you already hold: send one client a benchmark against anonymized peers of their size, and one an "I saw this and thought of you" note about something they have not asked about.
- Try Gregg's two-by-one for a month: two genuinely personal sentences once a week in a client email. If you do not follow the thing they follow, say so and ask why they care about it rather than faking familiarity.
