## Why this episode

You are two years into a finance or professional services job, you are technically good, and it is not obviously translating into better work. Leon Brujis has codified about ten lessons from his own years as an investment banking analyst and private equity associate, and most of them are about everything other than the modeling — who your work is actually for, when to stop polishing, how to check your own output, and why blunt feedback is the kind version. Two of the lessons come from his own mistakes.

## Guest

Leon Brujis is a partner at Palladium Equity Partners, described in the episode's introduction as the oldest minority-owned private equity buyout firm in the industry, with over $3 billion in assets under management. He came up as an investment banking analyst and then a private equity associate, and this is his third appearance on Branch Out. ## Key ideas

**Finance is an apprenticeship business, which makes your supervisor the job rather than an obstacle to it.** Brujis's framing is that the junior's role is to support the team, and the operational version of that is making your supervisor look good, because that person has the most influence on your assignments and progression early on. He is explicit that showing up to build a resume is the wrong mindset.

**Being good is not enough, and the missing skill is political fluency.** Every job has politics, every organization has norms about what gets rewarded, and he tells ambitious juniors to play the game rather than pretend it does not exist. Initiative is welcome — his word for the skill is ring-fencing it, so that reaching above your level does not read as stepping out of bounds.

**Perfectionism is the most expensive habit a junior can have.** The instruction is to get the work as good as you can as fast as you can, then show an early draft and ask whether you are on the right track. His reasoning is that you do not yet know what your supervisor wants, and the 80-20 arithmetic is brutal: the first 80% of the work costs 20% of the time, and the last stretch costs the rest. Alex's version is the analyst who models all night in a direction that was wrong from the start.

**Checking your work is a mechanical discipline, not a personality trait.** Brujis estimates 80% of analysts get the attention-to-detail comment in their first annual review, himself included. The fix is procedural: step away and do something else before you look again, then change the format — print it, PDF it, read it on your phone — and go in assuming you are biased toward believing your own work is right.

**Build several mentors at different levels, and weight the ones inside your own team.** Senior mentors give you the long view; experienced analysts and associates teach you how the work is actually done. But the mentors who sit in your group are the ones who influence your promotion and your bonus, and Brujis notes that more distant people are often easier to approach because they expect the ask.

**Ask for feedback so often that the annual review contains nothing new.** He frames withholding correction as the real unkindness, because an uncorrected mistake hardens into a habit. The two lines he keeps are that directness is kindness and that people want to know the truth. He closes on delivery: answer first, lead with the punchline, keep it short, and let the other person peel the onion.

## Try this

- On your next substantial assignment, stop when the analysis is directionally complete rather than finished, send it, and ask one question: am I on the right track? Brujis's argument is that the polish you apply before that question is the polish most likely to be thrown away.
- Build a format change into how you check your work. Print the analysis, export it to PDF, or read the email on your phone twenty minutes after writing it. The point is not more care, it is different eyes.
- Pick two people to ask for coffee this month and make sure one of them is inside your own group. Brujis's method was walking into the office and asking directly; the in-team mentor is the one who has a say in your review.
