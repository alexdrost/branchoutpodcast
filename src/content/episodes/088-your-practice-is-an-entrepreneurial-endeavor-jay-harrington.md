## Why this episode

You keep telling yourself you will start on business development when things slow down, and things never slow down. Jay Harrington practiced law at two large firms before building his own, and he hands you copyable systems rather than encouragement: a contact list sized to the business days in a month, and a LinkedIn loop that escalates posts into articles and books. He also explains why the billable hour wins by default, and what a single protected hour a day is actually worth.

## Guest

Jay Harrington is a consultant, coach and strategist who works with lawyers and law firms on marketing and business development. He started practicing at Skadden, Arps in Chicago on September 17, 2001, was reassigned from M&A to corporate restructuring days before he arrived, later moved to Foley & Lardner in Detroit, and then built his own restructuring boutique through the automotive crisis before turning to consulting in 2012. He has written four books, among them *The Productivity Pivot*, *The Essential Associate*, and *One of a Kind*. Find him on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/jayharrington).

## Key ideas

**Autonomy, not compensation, is the best predictor of satisfaction — and in private practice you only get it by owning clients.** Harrington describes the bonus treadmill losing its pull and control of his own circumstances replacing it, and points to research on lawyers specifically. The test he uses is whether you could take the relationships with you if everything fell apart.

**Hyperbolic discounting explains why the billable hour always wins.** Humans overweight short-term rewards even when the long-term ones are far larger, and billable hours are abundant, incentivized and sitting in front of you every day. Any practice-building system has to be built against that gradient rather than assume good intentions will hold.

**Charlie Munger's rule is the whole prescription: treat yourself as your own most important client and sell yourself one hour a day.** Roughly ten percent of your time is enough; you do not need a majority of it. Book the hour where you are most productive and least required to be responsive — usually before the inputs start — and spend the first ones on systems, delegating, eliminating, simplifying and automating, because the overwhelm blocking business development is a process problem.

**Every niche has an ecosystem of attention, and once you name the niche the marketing plan writes itself.** Associations, conferences, publications, shared advisors. Harrington's own firm skipped the tier-one auto suppliers every other Detroit firm was chasing and went after GM and Chrysler dealers losing their franchises, and roughly 30 dealership clients came out of showing up where nobody else did. He says he has never seen a proposed niche that was too small — the objection is fear of missing out, not market size.

**Business development is one-to-one; marketing is one-to-many; chasing active demand is neither.** His system is 20 to 25 key contacts, matched to the number of business days in a month, with one touch per day that is an insight, an introduction, or an invitation — never a request for work. By the time you hear about the deal, the ship has sailed; you needed to have been present seven or eight months earlier.

**Thought leadership is the most effective marketing precisely because it costs the most time.** That expense is why most people skip it and why the opening exists. Harrington's shift was to stop using LinkedIn to promote his blog and start using it as the blog, so short posts test ideas against his audience and the ones that land become published articles or books. The reporters, event organizers and publishers handing out high-reputation opportunities are on the same platform hunting for thought leaders.

## Try this

- Find the block where you are most productive and least required to be responsive, and put a recurring 30-to-60 minute appointment with yourself there for the next month. Spend the first two sessions delegating, eliminating or automating one recurring task rather than marketing.
- Write out 20 to 25 key contacts — internal colleagues included, since the people staffing you now become in-house counsel later — and reach out to exactly one per business day with an insight, an introduction or an invitation.
- Post one short idea natively on LinkedIn each week for a month. Take the one that draws the most response and expand it into a long-form article you pitch somewhere your target market already reads.
