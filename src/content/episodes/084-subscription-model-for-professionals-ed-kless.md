## Why this episode

You already suspect the billable hour is a bad fit for what your firm actually does, and you have no idea what would replace it or what to say when a partner asks about the one-off engagement. Ed Kless has a working definition of subscription pricing, a rule of thumb for what happens to your price and your headcount of customers, and an answer to the two objections you will hear first. He is arguing against the profession's default here, not summarizing it.

## Guest

He co-hosts The Soul of Enterprise with Ron Baker, with whom he helped develop value pricing, and hosts the Sage Advice podcast. ## Key ideas

**A professional firm is not a service firm, and calling it one is why it still bills by the hour.** Kless's test is physical: a service organization requires someone to show up at a location — the airport, the hotel, the McDonald's. Knowledge travels inside the person who holds it, so what these firms sell is access to or transfer of knowledge, and that requires a different business model.

**Measuring knowledge in hours is a category error, not a pricing preference.** His image is a ruler stuck into a Thanksgiving turkey to judge doneness: four inches is an answer, and it is meaningless. The customer wants the answer, and whether it took ten minutes or ten days does not change what they bought.

**"Client" carries a Latin inheritance the profession should want to shed.** Kless, son of a Latin teacher, points out that client means one who leans — the same root as incline, decline and recline — a person to be propped up and fixed. Customer is Anglo-Saxon and means one whose custom it is to come back. He argues the second word describes the relationship a firm should actually be selling.

**Subscription is not the same thing as doing next year's tax return.** His working definition, developed with Ron Baker: periodic recurring payments for ever-increasing value and serial transformations. Doing the same return every year is re-occurring. Knowing a customer's child turns 17 and offering the financial aid information before they ask is the transformation he means.

**Expect roughly three to four times the pricing power on about half the customers.** Kless is direct that dividing the current fee by twelve is doing it wrong. Move perhaps 50 of 100 customers onto the model at a materially higher price, and the result is less work for the same money or more. What is in scope is whatever you are extraordinarily capable of doing; if you would do a given engagement once a decade, be the quarterback and refer it out.

**Month-to-month with 30 days' notice is the offer, and the cancel button is the guarantee.** Requiring a year signals you do not trust your own value, and it traps you with the wrong customers. The exit runs both directions. Pricing under this model also shifts: Kless has moved from his old value-pricing mantra of pricing the customer to pricing the portfolio, spreading contingent work like audit representation actuarially across the whole risk pool.

## Try this

- Sort your customer list into the half you would keep if you could only keep half, then write a monthly price for the top five at three to four times what they pay you now. You do not have to send it. The exercise is finding out whether you believe the number.
- Rewrite the term clause in your standard engagement letter to month-to-month with 30 days' notice on both sides. Then use it once this quarter, in the direction Kless says people forget: to end a relationship that is not working.
- Take one thing you currently bill separately and treat contingently — audit representation is his example — and work out what share of your customers actually needed it last year. That percentage is the input for pricing it into a subscription across the whole pool.
