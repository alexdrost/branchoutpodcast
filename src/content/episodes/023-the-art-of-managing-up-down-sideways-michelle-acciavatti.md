## Why this episode

You have been coached on handling your boss and developing your team, and probably never on what to do with the people sitting at your level. Michelle Acciavatti argues that is the expensive gap: peers are who tell you the truth once seniority has quietly removed everyone else who would, and a Harvard Business Review study she cites found roughly a third of C-suite executives passed over for CEO lost out on relationships. Useful if you have a difficult boss, a team you avoid giving feedback to, or no idea when you last called a peer for no reason.

## Guest

Michelle Acciavatti is a market leader with Willis Towers Watson and an actuary by training, a profession she describes as full of introverts who would rather not lead with their feelings. She credits most of the frameworks she uses to clients rather than to herself, including the three-vantage-point model she applies to managing up. She is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/michelleacciavatti/).

## Key ideas

**Peer relationships are the highest-return investment available in a career.** Acciavatti puts them above every other kind, because the further you advance the less you personally deliver and the more you depend on partnerships you cannot order into existence. She backs it with the study on executives who reached the C-suite and stalled there.

**Nobody works for you, which makes "managing down" the wrong verb.** People operate on free will; what you actually owe them is development. Her stated goal is to build people who can be her, on the theory that you cannot move on to anything else until someone can replace you.

**Assume the difficult person is carrying something you cannot see.** Acciavatti starts from the position that 99.9 percent of people are good and rational, and reaches for the Covey story about the father on the bus. Drost adds that he has both worked for the difficult person and been him.

**Trust is what turns a hard conversation into an ordinary one.** Feedback lands badly when the relationship has not been built, and defensiveness is the natural response to someone whose motives you cannot read. She borrows a client's phrasing rule — "how to" and "I wish" in place of "you didn't" — as the mechanical version of the same idea.

**Caring cannot be faked, and it costs less time than you are assuming.** Two minutes before and after a call, an email that explicitly needs no reply, an unscheduled three-minute phone call that lands as a small event because nobody does it anymore. Her rule is not to let perfect get in the way of good.

**Recruit peers whose strengths are the opposite of yours.** Acciavatti describes herself as a big-idea person who needs a detail person next to her asking for the twenty-five answers required to execute. The people you serve come at problems from angles you do not, so your bench should too.

## Try this

- Name three peers you came up with and have not spoken to in six months. Call one of them this week without scheduling it and without anything to ask for.
- Before your next piece of work goes to a senior person, write one line each on what they are dealing with as a person, in their role, and from the company — then rework the material so it is ready to forward wherever they send it next.
- In your next feedback conversation, replace every "you didn't" with an "I wish" or a "how to" and notice what it does to the temperature of the room.
