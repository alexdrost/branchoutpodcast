## Why this episode

You have restarted the same morning routine four times. Mike Alberts is the counterexample to the advice you keep failing at: he tried waking at 5am to train before work, found his head was already in his inbox, and rebuilt the day backwards — hard work first, workout second. If your routine keeps collapsing, this episode argues the problem may be the prescribed sequence rather than your discipline.

## Guest

Mike Alberts is Vice President with Cohere Capital, a Boston-based growth-focused private equity firm investing in middle-market businesses. He describes spending the better part of a decade reading the books and testing systems on himself, and the episode is framed around training for an Ironman he signed up for with his fiancée while running deals. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/albertsmike).

## Key ideas

**The work-out-first template failed Alberts for a reason he can name, not from weak discipline.** Waking at 4:30 or 5 to train, he spent the whole session thinking about a full inbox and the deals he had to review. The workout was hollow, so the next day he skipped it and went straight to the desk, and the pattern spiraled from there.

**Inverting the order fixed it: a timed block of the work he dislikes, then the workout.** Alberts front-loads the things that accumulate — an NDA to negotiate, a document to mark up, CRM and calendar admin — into an hour or two with a timer on it. The workout stops being the thing standing between him and his day and becomes the break he is moving toward. Alex Drost says on air that the opposite is true for him: if he does not hit the gym in the morning, it does not happen.

**High achievers design for a 30-hour day, miss, and then feel worse than if they had planned nothing.** Twelve hours of work, dinner with family, eight hours of sleep, a workout, Netflix. Alberts' point is that the shortfall is not neutral — it compounds into the next day, which is how a routine dies.

**Gratitude only worked once it got small.** Writing down that he has good friends and an amazing fiancée felt like failing the exercise; the practice started working when the entries became a ripe avocado, particularly good coffee, a call with a friend the night before. Specific and small is what he can actually reach for on a day when nothing feels good.

**When you keep missing, the useful question is diagnostic rather than moral.** Not "why am I failing" but: wrong time of day, wrong gym, too far to travel, too many habits stacked at once. Alberts pairs that with a check in the other direction — he admits work is his standing excuse, and that taking a step back means separating a real obstacle from a convenient one.

**Measurement and comparison can both cost more than they return.** A week of logging his day in 30-minute increments told him the logging itself was eating 30 minutes a day. He is equally direct that watching other people's routines on LinkedIn and Instagram makes it harder, not easier, to find the one that fits you.

## Try this

- Pick the task you have been avoiding — the markup, the CRM entries, the email that is four weeks old — and put it in a timed block first thing tomorrow, with an actual timer. Decide the categories the night before so you do not drift into the interesting deal instead, and put the workout immediately after the timer goes off.
- Shrink your gratitude entries to Alberts' size. Three specific things from the last 24 hours, at the scale of a ripe avocado or a good cup of coffee, not the standing facts of your life.
- The next time you break a streak, do not restart it. Run four questions first — wrong hour, wrong location, too much travel, too many habits at once — then change exactly one variable and try again. Be honest about whether work was a real constraint or the excuse you always reach for.
