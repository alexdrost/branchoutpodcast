## Why this episode

You are busy enough that learning has quietly become the thing you do when there is time, which means never. Elliott Holland's answer is not a discipline problem but a scoring problem: if the only outcome you count as a win is the closed deal, then most of what you do all week generates nothing, and the people beating you are the ones harvesting data from failure at twice your volume. Useful if you own a book of business, run outbound, or have caught yourself waiting for your firm to pay for training.

## Guest

Elliott Holland is founder and managing director of Guardian Due Diligence, where he runs financial diligence for individual buyers acquiring businesses in the one-to-ten-million-dollar range. He came up in financial services, worked at Accenture in 2007, and spent time in the sales organization at Workday, which is where his framing of quotas as experiments comes from. This is his second appearance on Branch Out. Find him at [linkedin.com/in/elliottholland](https://www.linkedin.com/in/elliottholland/).

## Key ideas

**Every effort should be set up as an experiment, so that a bad result still returns something.** Holland's scientific background gives him the frame: you design it appropriately, and then you either get the outcome you sought or you get the lesson. Ten calls with one script that close nothing still tell you the script does not work on that audience. A hundred calls with ten scripts tell you which one does.

**A narrow definition of winning does not make you disciplined, it makes you beatable.** He is direct about it: if the only win is ten calls producing ten customers, you will get beaten in the market. The people who win are deliberately spending effort on things they suspect will fail, because the data is worth the spend.

**The person who wins the quarter is almost always the person who failed the most.** Holland's version comes from quarterly business reviews, where the rep nobody expected shows up with the best number. Asked what they did, the answer is either that they failed enough at one approach to switch to another, or that they simply ate twice as much failure as everyone else.

**Efficiency and creativity solve different problems, and confusing them wastes a year.** If a task takes ten hours and should take five, that is an efficiency problem worth working. A bad product with a bad script in a bad market segment is not — no amount of process improvement rescues it, and Holland says flatly that you are not going to efficient yourself out of it.

**Nobody is going to fund your development, and that is rational, not unfair.** He hears "my company won't pay" from corporate friends constantly and thinks the complaint answers itself: your employer has no incentive to make you worth more money elsewhere. His claim is that people who pay for their own coaching, courses and masterminds are so rare that spending your own dollars and time puts you in the top 5%.

**Growth belongs in the category of things whose absence kills you slowly.** The analogy is diet and exercise: skip it for a week or a month and nothing happens, skip it for ten years and there are real problems. He grants one honest exception — if you are still solving for rent, you are legitimately in execution mode and the Maslow argument applies.

## Try this

- The next time a prospect turns you down, say out loud that the sales portion of the call is over, then ask two questions: was there anything you could have done, and was there a sale to be had at all. Holland's point is that the second question tells you whether you lost or were never playing.
- Take the one pitch or outreach script you have been running unchanged and write three variants of it this week. Assign them across your next batch of conversations so the failures come back sorted rather than as one undifferentiated no.
- Pick one thing you have been waiting for your firm to pay for — a coach, a course, a mastermind, a designation — and buy it yourself this quarter. If that is too big a jump, start with his free version: download five whitepapers from adjacent industries this week and read them in one sitting on the weekend.
