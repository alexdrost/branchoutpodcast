## Why this episode

Your firm has hired for diversity and can produce the numbers to prove it, and the same handful of people still make every decision. Charmone Adams names the gap and gives it a test you can apply to your next meeting. He also argues, more uncomfortably, that you cannot run an inclusive practice on Monday if your golf group and your kids' birthday parties all look like you.

## Guest

Charmone Adams is an advisory services senior manager with Grant Thornton, working with clients across nationalities, regions, and religions. He describes deliberately handing associates and senior associates the platform to present and lead work in front of leadership rather than doing it himself. He also draws on a personal mentor who coached him the same way. Find him on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/charmone-adams-cpa-01513a11/).

## Key ideas

**Organizations reliably achieve diversity and stop short of inclusion.** Adams calls inclusion the secret sauce and the thing most firms miss: you can assemble a diverse room and still have a much less diverse subset making every call in it. His framing is the difference between being invited to the dance and being asked to dance.

**Inclusion is an act of assignment, not an attitude.** In practice it means naming someone else to present to the board, or pulling a person into a meeting they are not normally in because they will see something you will not. Adams says he does this regardless of whether the person is an associate or a senior associate.

**The case can be made in the language of finance, and Adams thinks it should be.** A different perspective often gets to the same result faster and cheaper, which streamlines process and saves expense. That, in his account, is what innovation actually is.

**You cannot practice this at work and skip it at home.** Adams ties the professional habit to who he golfs with, whose birthday parties he attends, and the friend groups his son keeps. His argument is that an inclusive practice built on a homogeneous life is not authentic, and clients can tell.

**Personal change should be planned on a three-year horizon.** Adams treats D&I work as a personal growth journey and says a three-year plan with accountability attached is more realistic than the two-week version most people commit to and abandon. Alex adds the pattern he sees in his own written goals: people overestimate what changes quickly and underestimate what changes over years.

## Try this

- Take the next piece of work you would have presented to leadership yourself and hand it to the person who did it. Tell them a week ahead so they can prepare, and let them answer the questions.
- Name one person who is not normally in your Thursday meeting and invite them specifically because they will see something you cannot, then ask them directly what they noticed.
- Find thirty minutes in the next week to write down three things about yourself — habits, mindsets, behaviors — you want changed three years from now. Put the list where you will see it, and attach one person who will ask you about it.
