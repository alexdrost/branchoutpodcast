## Why this episode

You are advising on a deal, or sitting inside one, and the integration plan has no line for how people will actually behave once it is announced. Jennifer Fondrevay went through three multibillion-dollar acquisitions as a marketer and argues that what gets read as resistance is grief — people mourning a future that is no longer going to happen. If you advise clients through transactions, she also makes a specific case for what your job is in the months after signing, most of it unbilled.

## Guest

Jennifer Fondrevay is the founder of Day1 Ready, a consultancy that advises business leaders, owners and C-suite executives on the human capital challenges of M&A. She spent 25 years in marketing, and in the last decade of that career went through three separate multibillion-dollar acquisitions, beginning with Nokia's purchase of the digital mapping company Navteq in 2008. She wrote *Now What?*, a survivor's handbook built on interviews with CEOs, CFOs and private equity investors and illustrated with ten caricatures of the personalities that surface under deal pressure. Find her on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/jennifer-fondrevay/).

## Key ideas

**Fondrevay calls mergers and acquisitions change tsunamis, and treats the stages of grief as the operative model.** A deal is typically the most dramatic change of someone's career. Everyone starts in denial and travels toward acceptance, but nobody takes the same route and the path is not linear — which is why a schedule that assumes uniform adjustment fails.

**What people are mourning is secondary ambiguous loss.** The term came from a CEO she interviewed whose mother was a grief counselor, and she says it named exactly what she had felt. The loss is intangible: a future you had envisioned that is no longer your expected future, with nothing yet in place to replace it. People also mourn certainty, familiarity, their boss, and the team they thought they would keep.

**Deals underperform on humility.** Asked what they learned from their disappointing transactions, the CEOs she interviewed said the same thing repeatedly — they wished they had come to the deal appreciating what the other side brought. The failure mode is "here's how we've done it, here's how you fit in" instead of co-creating, and Fondrevay is clear it can coexist with negotiating hard on terms.

**Red flags found in diligence do not get fixed after close.** Her reference point is marriage: nobody who went in expecting the other person to change was right. A business owner is not going to become a different person once the deal is signed, and she has heard CEOs describe walking away at exactly that recognition.

**The caricatures name failure modes people recognize instantly.** The former rock star had the Midas touch before the deal and cannot pivot when the metrics for success change; coddling makes it worse, so name the pivot, role-model it, and move on without them rather than let a toxic culture set. The dominatrix — the bully — looks effective early because results arrive, but fear burns out talent and the deal misses its valuation. The know it all kills the collaboration and recalibration phase, and appears on both sides of the transaction, not only the acquirer's.

**The advisor's role is outside observer and sounding board, and it is undervalued.** Fondrevay tells advisors to invite the vent, to ask the client what they loved about the job and whether they can see it in the new structure, and to talk people out of abandoning equity they have built because the grass looks greener. Everyone else is dealing with the same uncertainty.

## Try this

- Run a pre-mortem with the executive team before the next close: assume the deal is signed, then ask what could affect it, positive and negative. Write down where people diverge — the divergence is the output, not the solutions.
- Take the red flag you have been planning to deal with after closing and put the real question on the table now: is this a partnership problem or a fixable one? Decide before signing.
- If you advise, call the client whose firm just got acquired, invite them to vent, and ask two questions — what did you love about this job, and can you see it in the new structure? Do it without a bill attached.
