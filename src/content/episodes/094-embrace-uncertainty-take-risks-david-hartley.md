## Why this episode

You have been handed something you have never done before, and the reflex is to bluff until you catch up. David Hartley spent a decade in Big Four audit, a decade in technology, and six years running technology for a public company before taking over the advisory practice at a Top 100 CPA firm, and he says he is a more effective leader in the rooms where he knows least. If the training that made you precise is now making you rigid, this is the argument against expertise made from inside accounting.

## Guest

David Hartley leads the advisory services practice at Anders CPAs and Advisors, a Top 100 firm in St. Louis. He passed the CPA exam, spent almost a decade at Ernst & Young in audit and then technology, led technology for a public company for six years, and did virtual CIO work for middle-market companies before joining Anders three and a half years before this recording. In that stretch the firm doubled in size and the advisory practice tripled. [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/davehartley/)

## Key ideas

**Change gets harder as you get more senior, not easier.** Hartley expected his tolerance for uncertainty to rise with experience and found the opposite: once you are responsible for setting the tone and everyone is watching how you react, a transition costs more than it did when you were junior. He does not know anyone who is comfortable with it.

**A leader is more useful in the room where they have the least experience.** When Hartley knows the answer he focuses on his own contribution. When he does not, his contribution becomes managing the room — noticing who is dominating, getting people into the right spots, setting them up and supporting them. He argues that is the actual job.

**Accounting training handicaps people for leadership.** The profession raises you to believe there is one right answer, that missing it makes you wrong, and that you must be the expert on everything. Hartley says the further you get into a career the less that holds, and the transition to facilitating a group is the one accountants struggle with most.

**A first-day hire has something to teach you before lunch.** Hartley addresses new hires at Anders and tells them the historical pyramid, where knowledge and power sit at the top, no longer describes the firm. They arrive as digital natives trained on tools the partners do not use; the belief that "I'm nothing until I'm a partner" is the thing he is trying to dismantle.

**If your team never fails, it is not pushing hard enough.** Hartley pairs the push out of the comfort zone with explicit permission to fail — people who take on a stretch role know he is not going to prosecute them for it. Occasional failure is his evidence the team is taking appropriate risk.

**Watching for overwork is the leader's job, because most people will not raise their own hand.** He keeps dashboards of vacation balances and goes to people whose balance is climbing to ask what obligation is keeping them from stepping away. Self-awareness about stress is a learned skill, and he wants people learning it early, when the stakes are low.

## Try this

- Pick the next problem you are least qualified to solve and get yourself in that room without preparing an answer. Spend the meeting managing who is talking rather than talking.
- Before you assign the next task, write one sentence on why it exists and where it sits in the larger job, and say it out loud when you hand it over. On the receiving end, ask for that sentence before you start.
- Pull your team's vacation balances this week. Call anyone whose balance keeps climbing and ask what they think will fall over if they step away.
