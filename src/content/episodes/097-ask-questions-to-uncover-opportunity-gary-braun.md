## Why this episode

You have one meeting, you have been chasing it for three months, and you spend it establishing how much you know. Gary Braun's diagnosis, drawn from close to 400 client companies, is that the meeting was already lost — two surface questions in, you switched to telling. This episode is about what has to be true before you are allowed to present, and about getting a whole team to run the same sequence instead of watching one natural do it by instinct.

## Guest

Gary Braun co-founded Pivotal Advisors in 2008 with his brother; the firm helps small and mid-sized companies make sales consistent and repeatable. He came up through selling, was promoted into sales management, and says his first turn leading a team made it worse — he closed deals for his reps instead of coaching them. The company where he later ran sales grew from a million dollars in revenue to $400 million. [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/pagarybraun/)

## Key ideas

**If you say the value, you are a salesperson; if the buyer says it, it is true.** Braun's first sales boss gave him that line, and his whole method follows from it. Ask what a five percent lift in close rate would be worth and let the buyer do the arithmetic out loud — when they land on half a million dollars a year, that number is theirs, not yours.

**The most common failure across 400 companies is not a bad pitch, it is a fast one.** A couple of surface questions, then what he calls verbal vomit mode: everything you know about the service, delivered at someone whose problem you have not yet defined. Braun's alternative is to keep expanding — tell me more, how would that affect the company, who else is involved.

**Discovery is not finished until you have five things.** The current solution and what they like about it, the quantified impact of changing it, every decision maker, the timing, and the budget. Missing any one of them means the presentation you are about to give is a coin flip, and the meeting was spent on your material rather than their situation.

**Renaming sales does not change what you are doing.** Braun will run search-and-replace on his materials so a CPA firm never sees the word. He also thinks the aversion is unfounded: if you ask a client about problems they cannot fix themselves and suggest how to fix them, you are selling, and the discomfort belongs to the label rather than the act.

**Narrow the proactive effort, not the inbound.** His flooring-manufacturer client had three salespeople covering twelve markets that buy in completely different ways; cutting to three markets doubled revenue inside a year. Work that comes to you from outside the focus is still worth working — the constraint is on where you spend your own time.

**Documenting the process is the easy part; adoption is the job.** Getting a team onto the same steps produces a 17 to 19 percent lift, but only if someone rides along and debriefs against a checklist. Braun's accountability standard is four or five specific pieces of positive reinforcement for every correction, and he notes that most managers run exactly the inverse.

## Try this

- Write the five discovery requirements on a card — current solution, quantified impact, all decision makers, timing, budget — and take it into your next first meeting. If you leave with a gap, book the second meeting rather than presenting into it.
- Open your next first meeting by saying you do not yet know whether you are a fit, that today is about understanding their situation, and here is what the next step looks like if you are.
- Sit in on one colleague's client call this month and debrief it against a written checklist: did we set the agenda, were the right decision makers in the room, who did most of the talking, did we differentiate. Then count how many of your comments were positive and specific.
