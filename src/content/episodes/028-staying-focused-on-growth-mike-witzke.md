## Why this episode

You agree that professional development matters, and you have done none of it this quarter, because client work is louder than anything you were planning to do for yourself. Mike Witzke runs a law practice and still holds Monday mornings for it, and he is specific about what makes the block survive contact with a busy week: a goal broken into ninety-day pieces, and somebody outside your firm willing to ask why you have now missed three times running. Read this if your development plan is currently a set of year-end resolutions.

## Guest

Mike Witzke is a partner with McDonald Hopkins, a Cleveland-based law firm with more than 140 professionals. For several years he belonged to a mastermind group of lawyers from around the country that met quarterly — fly out Thursday, home Sunday — and required each member to write down a promise that was read back to the group at the next session. He signed up convinced he did not have the time. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/michaelwitzke/).

## Key ideas

**Growth has to be recurring blocked time you treat as sacred, not an annual resolution.** Witzke keeps Monday mornings clear of client meetings, calls and email, and lays the rest of the week onto the calendar from there. He is direct that if you do not defend the block, email and phone calls and other people's deadlines will take it, one small piece at a time.

**The goal you state is usually a proxy for the goal you have.** "I want to make more money" often decodes, two questions later, to wanting time with family — and those two goals imply opposite work patterns. Witzke's instruction is to peel back the onion before you set the target, because the root reason is what gets you up again after a bad month.

**Cut the goal into ninety-day pieces with a named first physical step.** His mastermind required each member to write down the specific thing they would do on Monday when they got home. Big goals fail because progress is invisible; ninety days is short enough to see whether anything moved.

**Your accountability partner should not be your business partner, your colleague, or your spouse.** People too close to you either share your blind spots or cannot bring themselves to ask the uncomfortable question. Witzke suggests looking at clients or someone with an unfamiliar vantage point. Drost adds the failure mode: a group where everyone reports and nobody pushes is not accountability, it is a status meeting.

**Everyone over 45 should have a mentor who is under 35 — mentor, not mentee.** Witzke picked this up online and endorses it. His reasoning is that a fundamental shift in thinking is underway right now, and if you are on the wrong side of that line you are not tapped into it.

**Feedback should be captured and left to percolate, not accepted or rejected in the room.** Witzke's honest description of the failure is nodding along while your head repeats that it will never work. Some of what you hear genuinely will not apply — but arguing back ends the flow of feedback permanently, so the move is a follow-up question now and the real evaluation later.

## Try this

- Block two hours on next Monday morning, name the block after the thing you are trying to get better at, and decline the first meeting request that lands on it. Before the block ends, write the ninety-day version of the goal and the one step you will take that afternoon.
- Ask one person outside your firm and outside your household to be your accountability partner, and hand them the question to ask you: what did you commit to last month, and did you do it? Witzke's point is that the discomfort is the mechanism.
- Write your questions down before your next feedback conversation, and hold yourself to answering only with follow-up questions. Save the explanation of your constraints for a different day, once you have let the criticism sit.
