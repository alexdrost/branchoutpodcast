## Why this episode

Your firm said it wanted a more diverse team and a year later nothing has moved. Christine Nowaczyk and Rich Grant, who sit with host Alex Drost on the ACG Global diversity task force, take the question apart from two different seats: corporate banking and private equity business development. If you are trying to work out which of your own actions changes anything, they separate the ones that do from the ones that only feel good, and they are candid about how draining the committee work itself is.

## Guest

Christine Nowaczyk is the Arizona Corporate Banking Executive for BOK Financial and a leader in the M&A community. She argues that the foundation for everything else is being demonstrably good at your craft, and she would rather use the word connecting than networking. Rich Grant is Director of Business Development at Northlane Capital Partners, a private equity fund investing in business services and healthcare service businesses, and a returning guest from Ep 010; he moved into the middle market from the media side by making himself a student of the community. Find them at [linkedin.com/in/christine-nowaczyk-a5464514](https://www.linkedin.com/in/christine-nowaczyk-a5464514/) and [linkedin.com/in/gorichgrant](https://www.linkedin.com/in/gorichgrant/).

## Key ideas

**The binding constraint is awareness, not a ceiling.** Grant's argument is that plenty of people capable of this work have no idea the work exists, so visibility has to precede recruiting. His own response is to go back to his alma mater and teach 22-year-old Black men how to approach named people in his network, before they have careers to leverage.

**Mentoring should move from one-on-one to community.** Grant wants the mentor's main deliverable to be access rather than advice: put the mentee into conversations with your whole network so they meet people they might recognize themselves in. He credits his own path to business development professionals who let him into the room and let him stay.

**Nowaczyk holds craft as the non-negotiable foundation.** Be good enough that people engage you for the value you add rather than how you look, and the cultural change follows from there. She is explicit that there is no route around the work it takes to be valuable inside your own industry.

**A token hire is the worst available outcome, and the fix is commitment at the top rather than a lower bar.** Grant separates the goal from the plan: a public statement or a policy amendment satisfies the moment and then loses to business goals. A commitment without actionable items is a wish.

**Recruiting without development buys four uncomfortable years and then an exit.** Grant's second ask is enablement. Develop people past the job they were hired for and they stay in the room; skip it and they conclude the room was never theirs.

**The diversity conversation collapses into a networking conversation, and all three of them notice it happening.** Nowaczyk reframes networking as connecting and being of service with no expectation of return. Drost's version is that a single introduction into a network someone had no route into can change a trajectory, and that this is something every listener can actually do.

## Try this

- Make one introduction in the next seven days that connects someone into a network they had no route into, and tell both sides why you made it.
- Recruit one mentor from outside your own industry, specifically someone who will ask why you do it that way. Nowaczyk's most useful mentors were the ones with no reason to accept her defaults.
- Sort your firm's stated diversity commitment into three lists: what you can do this quarter, what takes a year, and what sits outside your control. Then work only the first list. That is Grant's method, and it is how he keeps the task force work from stalling.
