## Why this episode

Your firm's growth still runs on a handful of partners having breakfast with bankers and lunch with lawyers — and the best of them are close to retiring. Gale Crosley's argument is that the market conditions which made that work are gone, and that growth has to become a leadership function with named owners and a pipeline behind it. This is what a firm with a real growth capability actually has in place.

## Guest

Gale Crosley began as an accounting major and CPA, auditing at Arthur Andersen and Price Waterhouse, then pivoted 180 degrees into technology sales, marketing and new product development at IBM for a dozen years, followed by roughly another decade with venture-backed startups. She started her consultancy after the dot-com bust and has spent the years since transforming CPA firms into high-growth environments; she says most of her clients are in the top 100, and that she has written 160 articles, all posted at crosleycompany.com. [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/galecrosley/)

## Key ideas

**Strategic growth means capturing markets, not clients — a net rather than a line.** Crosley's claim is that this is simultaneously the fastest, most efficient and most profitable growth, because time spent inside one market compounds: you learn the buyer, the buying behavior, and where buyers gather, and specialization lets you charge a premium.

**The profession went a hundred years without needing to specialize, which is why the muscle was never built.** She uses a lifecycle framing — go-go years, cash cow, fat cat — to argue that margins only started thinning about twenty years ago, and that the strategic imperative has now shifted to specialization, innovation and efficiency. Firms that miss the shift, in her phrasing, go the way of the buggy whip.

**The staffing shortage has switched the demand machine off, and demand does not switch back on.** Every scrap of bandwidth goes to fulfilling demand. Firms that just ride market conditions — the rollercoaster strategy — are exposed the moment conditions turn, because rebuilding demand takes years, not quarters.

**Firms build strategy inside-out until the loudest voice wins.** Crosley's alternative is outside-in: go into each target market, evaluate its business use cases first, and let those dictate which of the technology enablers — data analytics, AI, machine learning, RPA, cyber, continuous audit, blockchain — and which people you buy. Her example of one strategic combination: AI in audits for skilled nursing facilities.

**The knights-of-the-roundtable partnership has to give way to a corporate structure.** Her case is accountability, faster decisions and leadership development, none of which the roundtable produces. Underneath the slowness she names a structural conflict: the same person decides as an employee of the firm and as an owner of it, multiplied by however many partners are at the table.

**Audit is not the casualty of AI — she expects it to expand.** ESG and other non-financial attestation are, in her forecast, an explosion in assurance work, and breaking audit out from the rest of the firm makes it as interesting as tax and advisory rather than less.

## Try this

- Run a profitability analysis by service line and by market before you set next year's growth targets, so you stop selling more of what you lose money on.
- Name one market you will go deep in this year, then answer three questions in writing: who is the buyer, how do they buy, and where do they gather together. If you cannot answer all three, you are not specialized in it yet.
- Stand up an opportunity pipeline for business, not just a pipeline for people, and put a named leader on each industry and service line who is accountable for its growth and its financial health.
