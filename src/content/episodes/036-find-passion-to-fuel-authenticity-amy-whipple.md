## Why this episode

You keep being told to find your passion, you have not found it, and you have started to assume something is missing in you. Amy Whipple's account is that passion arrives sideways: through a fundraiser she never planned to run, a 5 a.m. walk she takes because of who walks with her, and years spent alongside family members who were dying. If you are waiting for a defining moment to reveal what you care about, this episode argues the moment is not coming and that the alternative is better.

## Guest

Amy Whipple is Director of Business Development for BDO, working out of the firm's Detroit office. She co-founded 100 Women Who Care and 100 Businesses Who Care, giving circles where members pool donations quarterly or twice a year and pitch nonprofits they care about. Her philanthropic work started when her aunt Mary Lou was diagnosed with ALS at 44; the family became one of the founding families behind an annual benefit for the ALS Therapy Development Institute, an effort Whipple says raised a couple million dollars for research. Find her at [linkedin.com/in/amywhipple](https://www.linkedin.com/in/amywhipple/).

## Key ideas

**Most passions arrive by accident, not by search.** Whipple had never been to a fundraiser before her aunt's diagnosis, and then found herself leading one. The exposure came first and the passion followed, which reverses the order most career advice assumes.

**When you cannot be passionate about a habit, anchor it to something you are passionate about.** Whipple walks at 5 a.m. because of the friend who lives three doors down, not because she likes exercise. The walk is the cost of the friendship, which turns it into a cost she is willing to pay.

**Making someone stand up and say why they care exposes passion that years of board service never surfaced.** In 100 Businesses Who Care, members are drawn at random to pitch a nonprofit on the spot. Whipple has watched company presidents who had raised money for that organization for years get choked up the first time they had to say out loud why it mattered.

**Sustained comfort is a warning, not a reward.** Whipple's phrasing is that when everything is hunky dory you become complacent. Alex Drost's version is that if life feels entirely easy, you have either stopped pushing or you are missing something in front of you.

**You cannot always be the giver, and receiving well is a separate skill.** During the months her cousin and her sister-in-law were both dying of cancer, Whipple had nothing left to give and had to learn to take graciously. Drost's caveat is that receiving must not curdle into an expectation that others owe you.

**Masks removed the signals people were reading, so checking on someone now has to be spoken.** Whipple noticed she could no longer smile at a cashier and be understood. Her conclusion is that the intent you used to convey with a face now has to be said out loud.

## Try this

- Take the habit you keep abandoning and attach a person to it this week. Name one friend, propose a standing time, and put the first session on the calendar before you talk yourself out of it.
- Next time you ask someone for their story and they start at the last five years, push once into the part they glossed over. Whipple's recruiting version of this was to refuse the highlight reel and ask about the thing they mentioned in half a sentence.
- Do one unrequested thing for somebody in the next seven days: make a meal, send a handwritten card, or take a task off their plate. That is Drost's call to action in the episode, and it is deliberately small.
