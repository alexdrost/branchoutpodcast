## Why this episode

You already believe diversity is good, and that belief has never once told you what to do on Monday. Ernie Lyles thinks that is the whole problem: agreement is cheap because nobody shares a definition, so he rebuilds the argument in the language you actually make decisions in, which is optimization and return. If you have to sell this internally to people who evaluate everything as an investment, this is the version that survives the meeting.

## Guest

Ernie Lyles is founder and CEO of The HiGro Group, a New York private investment firm he has been building for five years. He grew up in Shepherdstown, West Virginia, went to Howard University School of Law, and taught himself financial modeling by sitting in on business school classes without credit. A 2005 summer internship at a global investment bank turned into ten years there; by the time he left, two people were left from his analyst class. Find him at [linkedin.com/in/ernest-lyles-6470572](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ernest-lyles-6470572/).

## Key ideas

**Everyone agrees on diversity and the agreement dissolves the moment you ask how.** Lyles puts it at roughly 90% of people saying yes in the abstract, then backing off at the first implementation question. His conclusion is that definitional work is not a preamble to the real conversation, it is the precondition for having one.

**Homogeneous firms win, and pretending otherwise costs you the argument.** He volunteers the counterexample before the host can raise it: there are plenty of homogeneous, financially successful people. The claim he will defend is narrower and harder to dodge — that homogeneity is suboptimal, and that suboptimal compounds into complacency and irrelevance as the world changes around you.

**Race and gender are necessary and nowhere near sufficient as a definition.** Socioeconomic background, geography, education and ideas all belong in it, and Lyles notes that lumping any group into one category hides the differences that matter. He is candid that even his own favorite opener — where are you from — carries biases of its own.

**Ambition and capability do not open the door; someone senior has to open it before the proof exists.** He describes teaching himself Excel with no credit and no mandate, then says plainly that it still required leaders willing to see past the missing Ivy and the law degree. He treats his own hire as the underwriting case: payback on an associate runs two to three years, he stayed ten, and 60 to 70% of his class was gone in three.

**School brand does not predict performance, and the pedigree filter is already breaking down.** His best analyst and his worst analyst both came from Yale; Indiana University hires performed just as well. He points out that state-school analysts, women returning from gap years and Southern accents arrived on Wall Street partly because privileged candidates left for other industries, and the financial system has worked better since, not worse.

**This is not a hiring program, so stop scoping it as one.** Lyles extends the same question to which regions you do business in, which management teams you back and which law firms you retain. He also insists on the time horizon: react to the news cycle if you must, but true alpha is generated over the long term.

## Try this

- In your next three introductory conversations, drop the firm-history question and ask where the person is actually from and how they got here. Lyles says he tunes out résumé recitals because two thousand other people have the same one; the point is to surface the characteristic that is theirs alone.
- Name one person already inside your building who sits outside the traditional promotion track — his example is the controller who has never missed a number — and write down the specific gap between where they are and a deal-side seat. Then decide this quarter whether you are willing to close it.
- Pull your last twenty engagements and list the regions, the management teams and the outside law firms. If diversity work in your firm has only ever been a hiring conversation, that list is where the rest of it lives.
