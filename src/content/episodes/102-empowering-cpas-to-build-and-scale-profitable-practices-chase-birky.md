## Why this episode

If you own a firm and still carry a heavy client-service load, this episode argues that trade-off is the reason your career paths are thin and your average tenure sits under three years. Chase Birky's alternative flips who the customer is: the CPA buys from the firm, and the client buys from the CPA. Whether or not you would ever join a platform, the diagnosis is worth arguing with.

## Guest

Chase Birky is co-founder and CEO of Dark Horse CPAs, which he describes as a platform CPA firm — a category he says his firm created. He built a practice from scratch before this, at a personal cost he calls almost irreparable, and pivoted the existing tech-forward firm into the platform model in 2019. He was licensed in California under the pre-150-hour rules and now lives in Nevada. [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/chasebirky/)

## Key ideas

**Birky's central claim is that the practitioner-owner model is the defect, not a feature.** Owners with a heavy hand in client service cannot put that time into their people or their firm, and he points to the average tenure below three years and to poor employment experiences as the evidence showing up downstream.

**Dark Horse inverts the customer relationship: the CPA is the firm's customer, and the client is the CPA's.** Everything the platform builds — marketing, branding, technology, staffing — is infrastructure a practitioner would otherwise assemble alone. His framing is that everyone is building the same wheel separately.

**Aligned economics make top-down control unnecessary.** Because principals share in net profit, Birky argues the machinery a traditional partnership needs to police work volume and quality has nothing to do. Principals take 45% to 50% of gross, against the traditional third-partner-comp, third-staffing, third-overhead accounting split.

**Wanting your name on the door is ego, and it is separable from autonomy.** He splits the desire for a namesake firm and total creative direction — which he says the platform will not solve for — from the autonomy that actually changes your life: how you serve clients, and how the practice fits around your family.

**Burnout is a bad barometer for your next job.** His warning is against taking the next opportunity that merely looks better: a desperate decision from a desperate situation. He is candid that his own first accelerators came from exactly that place, and that it worked out for Dark Horse.

**AI augments rather than replaces, but today's compliance margins are the trap.** Advisory is a blank slate with too many decision points to program; compliance is where if-this-then-that logic bites. Meanwhile the talent shortage is propping up tax prep pricing, and Birky's argument is that firms will get high on the hog for a couple of years while non-CPA players move in and compress fees — which is why the systematized advisory offering has to be built now.

## Try this

- Take one service you deliver and quote it at double your current price on your next fit call. Birky's claim is that the discomfort is a habit inherited from your last firm, not a read on the market.
- If you are burnt out, answer two questions in writing before you open a single job posting: do you want to be in accounting, and do you want to be in public accounting. Only then look at options.
- Pick one advisory offering and spend two hours this month writing it up as a repeatable process — delivery steps, deliverable, price — so it does not live only in one or two people's heads.
