## Why this episode

You believe you are a connector. Thom Singer's follow-up question is the one that stings: name the last introduction you made that actually led to business. Most people cannot, and this episode is about the gap between meaning to help people and doing it — including why the return, when it comes, will arrive from someone you have never met.

## Guest

Thom Singer became a professional speaker and corporate trainer thirteen years ago, after a career doing marketing and sales for law firms, banks and consulting firms, where he started out training the lawyers and bankers on how to network. He hosts Speakernomics, the National Speakers Association podcast, and does business development coaching for lawyers and accountants. He says roughly 90% of his own work comes from word of mouth he cannot trace. Find him at [linkedin.com/in/thomsinger](https://www.linkedin.com/in/thomsinger/).

## Key ideas

**Almost everyone claims to connect people, and almost no one can produce an example.** Singer's test question is when you last made an introduction that led to actual business. The usual answer is a blank look followed by "I do it all the time." His verdict on the gap is that intent is not action, and that most of the time the introduction simply takes longer in your head than it does in reality.

**If networking is a second-tier priority, it will return second-tier results.** He rejects the idea that there is a sequence — coffee, then a handwritten note, then something else — that produces a network. People build relationships in different ways, and there is no magic fairy dust. The variable that actually predicts the outcome is where it sits on your priority list.

**Relationships are built through shared experiences, and a connection request is not one.** A like, a link, a share and a follow does not constitute a business relationship in Singer's framing. Attending the same conference is a shared experience. So is a recurring Zoom call with someone in a different city, which is how he and Alex met.

**Keeping score guarantees the conclusion that networking does not work, because the payer is rarely the person you helped.** Singer's account is that givers get noticed by adjacent observers — the people watching you help someone else — and those observers are the ones who send you work years later. He applies the same logic to referral fees: he turns down the 5%, asks to be referred to his own work instead, and notes that most people never follow through, but a couple do and it is worth more than the trinket.

**Associations are not dying, and the entry price is three or four years of showing up.** He argues that post-pandemic disconnectedness makes belonging more valuable, not less, and points to his own thirteen years in the National Speakers Association: three or four years to build friendships, another three or four to build a reputation. People who attend one conference and conclude they made no friends have misread the timescale.

**Leading with your elevator pitch teaches you to repel people.** Singer's advice is to know how to describe yourself concisely and then hold it, asking questions instead until the other person reciprocates. He extends the same principle to who you talk to: look up, sideways and down, and treat the vendors selling into your industry as the best contacts in the room, because they see across all your competitors.

## Try this

- Write down the last introduction you made that led to real business between two people. If nothing comes to mind, pick one pair you already know should meet and send that email before the end of the day. Singer's point is that it takes minutes, and that after three weeks it gets awkward to raise.
- Publish your own terms for people who want to pick your brain and then say yes to all of them. His version was 7:30 a.m. at the Starbucks near his house, on the outskirts of town, which weeded out half the requests without him having to decline anything.
- If you are early in a firm, call three of its rainmakers this month and ask how they built their practice. The attorney Singer coached was certain this would look strange; two of the partners became his mentors, and he became known internally as the hungry one.
