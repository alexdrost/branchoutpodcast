## Why this episode

You have been treating burnout as a workload problem to push through. Randy Crabtree treats it as a health condition with causes and treatments, and he is specific about both — what uncontrolled stress does, what actually helped him, and what he would tell a senior manager who is done. If you are weighing whether to leave your firm, his argument is that leaving is usually the wrong first move.

## Guest

Randy Crabtree is co-founder and partner of Tri-Merit Specialty Tax Professionals and host of The Unique CPA podcast. He is a two-time finalist for Accounting Today's Top 100 Most Influential People in Accounting and presents regularly for national accounting associations and state CPA societies. Nine years before this recording he had a stroke; today his speaking work covers mental health in the profession alongside tax credits and incentives. ## Key ideas

**Crabtree defines burnout as a form of mental illness produced by uncontrolled stress — and he flags that this is his own definition, not a clinical one.** Stress itself, in his account, is functional: it is what makes you brake for the animal in the road. The damaging version is the deadline-after-deadline kind you have no control over.

**The stroke came first; the years that followed were driven by fear of a second one.** He does not attribute the stroke to his work. What he describes afterward is roughly five years of depression, PTSD and panic attacks set off by ordinary sensations — a twinge, a moment of dizziness — that he read as the beginning of another stroke. That aftermath is what he equates to professional burnout.

**Not every counselor is right for you, and stopping after a bad fit is the mistake.** The first one told him he could not control what he was worried about, so he should stop worrying; he answers that CPAs are trained to control things and to be the one with the client's answers. It took a third counselor before something shifted, and he credits the counseling — not the private ritual he also describes — with ending it.

**Identity attachment to a title kept him in a role he says was never right for him.** He stayed managing partner for three years while struggling, and looking back says the job did not suit him even before the stroke. He now describes his identity as education, and he moved to that full time inside the business rather than leaving it.

**Mid-career burnout usually calls for a role conversation, not a job search.** His advice is to stop hiding what you dislike, tell your supervisor, and ask to move service line or industry niche — then go try to bring in one client in the niche you actually care about.

**Structure beats resolve, at the day level and the firm level.** He lays out a three-part end-of-day shutdown: bookmark exactly where you will restart tomorrow, decide the specific thing you will do instead tonight, and perform a physical ritual that signals the day is closed. At firm level he points to Courtney DeRonde's firm, which capped tax season at 45 hours a week and, by his account, got more work done, billed more hours and was more profitable than the year before.

## Try this

- Build tonight's shutdown in three parts: write down the exact task you will restart on tomorrow, name what you are doing instead this evening, and pick a physical signal — closing the laptop, fifteen pushups, a few minutes of meditation — that ends the day.
- Put two 15-to-20 minute walks on next week's calendar as real appointments, and leave your phone on your desk for both.
- Before you touch your resume, book 30 minutes with your supervisor and name one service line or industry niche you want to move toward. Bring the name of one prospect in that niche to the conversation.
