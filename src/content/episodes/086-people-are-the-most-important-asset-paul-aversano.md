## Why this episode

Your calendar has filled with account teams, penetration strategy sessions and internal reporting, while the client and staff relationships that actually generate the revenue get whatever is left. Paul Aversano settled that trade-off by putting roughly 90% of his time on clients and people and delegating the rest of the business. This episode is what that decision costs, and what it buys.

## Guest

Paul Aversano is a Managing Director in Transaction Advisory and Global Practice Leader at Alvarez & Marsal. He joined a little over 16 years ago to co-found the firm's transaction advisory group, which he now leads globally. Before that he spent roughly 15 years in the Big Four — about a decade at Arthur Andersen, then partner at EY running its New York middle-market private equity practice — and he estimates he has worked on more than a thousand deals across 25-plus years in the business. [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/aversanopaul/)

## Key ideas

**A professional services firm sells people, so churn cancels out a strong year.** Aversano's framing is that the firm manufactures nothing, which makes hiring and retention the product rather than overhead. Outstanding financial results delivered on top of high turnover, in his view, are not a real win and are not sustainable.

**Retention rests on three things, and pay is only part of one of them.** Liking the work, liking the people you do it with, and believing the organization treats you fairly — across compensation, but also learning, development and advancement. Aversano applies the test to himself and coaches younger partners to apply it to their own careers.

**If it does not touch clients or people, a practice leader should not be in the room.** He describes firms drifting inward: twenty-person planning meetings to go after an elephant account, when someone could pick up the phone and call them. His own split is about 90% clients and people, 10% running the business, and he says he wishes he could get rid of the 10%.

**Family comes before the client, and Aversano says it out loud.** He rejects the standard "client first" formulation and states the order as family, client, firm, division, self. He shares the philosophy openly and tells people who disagree that they may not be a good fit — which is the part most firms will not copy.

**Compensation is a behavior design problem, so work backwards from the behaviors you want.** He also splits the annual review into two conversations: performance first, with bonus numbers explicitly not yet known, and money later. Combine them and nobody hears the performance half.

**You do not have a relationship with a client until they value it too — and a real trusted advisor will tell them not to do the deal.** The test he uses when interviewing people who claim a network: would these people take your call on a personal level? The corollary is that a relationship strong enough to survive disagreement is a relationship you can use to escalate a material finding over a deal team's head.

## Try this

- Pull last month's calendar and tag every hour as client, people, or internal. If internal runs past 10%, pick three recurring meetings and name the person you are handing each one to this quarter.
- Split your next review cycle in two. Book the performance conversation now, with bonus numbers explicitly unavailable, and put the compensation conversation on a separate date at least a week later.
- Write down the ten client contacts you would call relationships, then phone three of them this week about something other than the engagement. The ones who take the call personally are your actual list.
