## Why this episode

Your firm agrees that cross-selling matters, has already bought a CRM, and still cannot tell you how many services the average client buys. Timothy Keith came at that problem from outside the industry, hired to implement CRM at an accounting firm without knowing what cross-selling meant in that context. His argument is that the obstacle is neither information nor software, but the fact that technical experts will not risk looking ignorant in front of a client, and that the fix is to design around that rather than talk people out of it.

## Guest

Timothy Keith is CEO and founder of Propense.ai, an AI cross-selling platform for professional services firms. He entered the industry about seven years ago when he was hired to implement CRM at Kaufman Rossin, a top-60 accounting firm in Florida, after consulting on CRM projects in technology, advertising, and consumer businesses and building a note-taking company out of school. Before building the product, his team ran roughly 50 interviews with growth leaders at top-50 firms. Find him on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/timothy-keith-17825855/).

## Key ideas

**CRM does not fit a partnership, because it was designed for a C-suite that wants pipeline reports.** Keith's framing is that 99 percent of organizations run on that hierarchy, while a firm is closer to a holding company of individual books of business. The systems work in a firm only when they are built around automation and adoption rather than around people logging in.

**About five percent of a firm's people originate sixty to seventy percent of the revenue, which makes this a problem about the other ninety-five.** Keith's rainmakers are technically expert and naturally comfortable selling, and they are doing something nobody else at the firm is doing. The work is figuring out what that is and giving everyone else visibility into it.

**The real barrier is emotional rather than informational.** Practitioners are used to being the smartest person in the room, and the ask puts them somewhere they might sound uninformed or get told no. Keith's line is that smart people do not like to feel dumb, and no dashboard solves that.

**Nobody at a firm can name every service the firm sells, and clients are buying those services somewhere else.** Keith made the mistake himself, arriving at an accounting firm assuming cross-selling meant selling more tax work to tax clients. He hears the other half of it from clients who mention hiring an outside consultancy for an ERP implementation their own firm already does.

**A client who buys one service is the client most likely to leave.** Keith's data scientists found single-service clients carry the highest risk, which reframes this as retention rather than growth. Alex Drost adds the competitive version: whoever finds the money left on the table also takes the relationship.

**Design around the practitioner instead of trying to change them.** Keith's premise is that the busy senior partner will not adopt new habits, so the platform should surface the recommendation to growth teams whether or not behavior changes. His build sequence is four pillars in order: align incentives, then the system, then accountability, then empathy for how hard the change actually is.

## Try this

- Run one report this week: average number of services per client, then the same figure for your top ten clients. Keith finds most firms cannot answer that question, and no target means anything until they can.
- Take his low-risk script into your next client meeting. Clients like you in this industry have been looking at cybersecurity, would you like me to set up a conversation with the partner down the hall? It claims no expertise of your own, and the worst available answer is a polite no.
- Put the cross-sell numbers on the agenda of an industry group or practice-line meeting that already exists on the calendar, rather than standing up a separate initiative that needs its own attendance.
