## Why this episode

You finished a ten-hour day and cannot say where the hours went, and personal time keeps losing to whatever arrived in the inbox. Ryan Cutter works a private equity deal calendar and argues the reverse of what that schedule implies: six productive hours beat sixteen moderate ones, and the thinking your job actually pays for happens away from the desk. This is a short conversation between two people at the same career stage, and its value is the specific defaults they set rather than any grand theory.

## Guest

Ryan Cutter is a senior associate at CID Capital, a middle market private equity firm. He was a college track and field runner, let the training lapse in his early professional years, and rebuilt a daily 30-minute habit in 2021. He journals a few times a week and rereads old entries as his way of checking whether he has grown. Find him at [linkedin.com/in/ryan-cutter-29635b84](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ryan-cutter-29635b84/).

## Key ideas

**Not being able to account for your day is a finding, not a defence.** Cutter's test is simple: name what you did on Tuesday. Most people can list two or three things and lose the rest. He reads that as activity substituting for intention, and it is the reason he now starts the day with coffee and five to ten minutes of nothing before opening email.

**Cutter's lion-and-cow framing is the durable idea here.** A lion lounges and thinks, then chases the gazelle full throttle, then goes back to lounging. A cow grazes at low intensity for sixteen hours. He argues deal work rewards the first pattern, and that busywork performed to be seen is waste — no good employer is counting your hours in the office.

**Personal time is an input to your work, not a reward for finishing it.** Cutter's position is that the creative thinking that makes a deal work, or starts a business, arrives during exercise and leisure rather than in an inbox. He puts the case to Alex directly: the idea for this podcast did not surface mid-email.

**Denying yourself small things converts into resentment at the people around you.** The examples he gives are twenty minutes of a book and a trip to the gym. His argument is that the cost of skipping them is not the lost activity, it is what you start to feel toward colleagues and family, and that damages a career more than the hour would have.

**Inbox zero every night is a habit worth breaking.** Cutter admits he was an inbox-zero person. He now says some nights you go home with mail unanswered, take six hours for bad television or a hobby, and come back sharper, because in most cases the timing of the reply does not change the outcome.

**Working methods decay the same way business models do.** His example is Derrick Rose, still efficient after knee surgeries but playing a different game than at 21. Twenty years of experience loses to a two-year-old company that found a better way, so Cutter treats revisiting how you work as ongoing rather than annual — he does not believe in resolutions, though he still uses the calendar turn to name goals.

## Try this

- Put a recurring 30-minute block in your calendar for tomorrow morning before email opens — Cutter's is 7:00 to 7:30 — and defend it for two weeks before deciding whether it works.
- Pick one weeknight this week and go home with mail still in the inbox. Note the next morning whether anything actually broke.
- Open a document tonight and write four or five sentences about where you are and what you are working on. Set a reminder for a year out to read it.
