## Why this episode

You have priced every working hour of your life and none of the rest of it. Leon LaBrecque sold hours as a lawyer and then as a CPA before he decided to stop, and he gives you a way to cost out the chores you still do yourself, including the distraction, the reward you buy afterwards for having done them, and the argument they start at home. If you feel busy and cannot say what the busyness bought, this is the accounting.

## Guest

Leon LaBrecque is Chief Growth Officer at Sequoia Financial Group and a former practicing lawyer and CPA. He built the human bandwidth idea about a decade before this conversation and turned it into a TEDx talk, borrowing the math from the way a carrier computes electronic bandwidth: widen the pipe slightly and throughput rises roughly ninefold, and noise crowds out signal the same way it does on tape. He also builds wooden boats, which he offers as the exception to his own rule. Find him at [linkedin.com/in/leonlabrecque](https://www.linkedin.com/in/leonlabrecque/).

## Key ideas

**Human bandwidth has four components and only two of them are levers.** LaBrecque names them time, money, happiness and love. Time and money are what you can operate directly; happiness and love are what the freed capacity fills with, and every small widening of the pipe multiplies what moves through it.

**Time management is an incoherent goal, because you cannot manage a fixed quantity.** You get 86,400 seconds and they are gone at midnight. What you can manage is the noise: the attention biases that make this month's crisis feel permanent, and the hours spent thinking about what you ought to be doing rather than doing it.

**You misconstrue what every chore costs, and the honest number is much larger.** Ask how long the grocery run takes and you will say ninety minutes; run a stopwatch and it is three hours with the deciding, the driving and the wandering included. LaBrecque's house-cleaning arithmetic adds the three hours at your rate, the bottle of wine you buy yourselves for having done it, and the happiness lost to sniping at each other while you clean.

**Buying someone else's time expands their bandwidth, not just yours.** The housekeeper, the oil change shop, the roadside service: each purchase widens someone else's pipe, and the effect propagates through the network. LaBrecque's point is that outsourcing is not a selfish act, and he distinguishes it sharply from doing something because it genuinely makes you happy.

**The endgame is to stop selling time at all and start selling ideas.** Hours are capped at 86,400 a day and priced by the market. Ideas are unlimited and nobody knows what they are worth, which is the whole argument for carving out the white space to have one.

**One percent a day beats a fifty percent resolution, because fifty percent never starts.** Ask someone to change half of something and they refuse. Ask for two minutes of meditation tomorrow and two minutes twenty seconds the day after, and the compounding does more than the ambition would have.

## Try this

- Price one chore you still do yourself: the hours at your real rate, plus the distraction time, plus whatever you buy yourself afterwards for having done it. Get a quote for having it done and put the two numbers side by side before you decide.
- Spend one sitting this week automating the money chores. Auto bill pay, automatic transfers into savings, and a single annual date on the calendar to review investments instead of checking them.
- Take the ten-year helicopter ride on paper. Where you are in ten years, where your kids are, what you will wish you had done more of. Then move one thing on next week's calendar so it matches the answer.
