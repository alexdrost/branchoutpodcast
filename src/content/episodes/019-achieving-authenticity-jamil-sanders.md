## Why this episode

You built an early career by copying people who looked successful, and the copies worked well enough that you never stopped to ask whether the life they produce is one you want. Jamil Sanders hit that collision and treats the way out as a process rather than a personality trait: block the time, write down who you are, find the gaps, close them with something specific on the calendar. If you have been feeling out of balance without being able to name the cause, he argues that feeling is the diagnostic, not the problem.

## Guest

Jamil Sanders is a manager with Key Bank's Private Client. He runs values-based conversations with clients, pushing them off chasing a number or an industry benchmark and toward what they actually want the back half of their life to look like — a reframe he says clients resist at first. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/jamil-a-sanders/).

## Key ideas

**Templates are fine as frameworks and dangerous as foundations.** Sanders likes templates and does not think you should reinvent the wheel. His distinction is where you put them: build the original foundation yourself, then borrow the structure. Careers that are entirely someone else's recipe eventually produce his Forrest Gump moment — you are still running and you no longer know why.

**In a business development role, authenticity is not a virtue but capital.** Sanders treats it as a working asset: the thing that drives results and the thing that determines whether relationships hold. His test case is the meeting opener. Ask how someone is doing while not caring about the answer and they can tell, which means the technique costs you more than skipping it would.

**Feeling out of balance is the reliable signal that you are off.** Sanders names professional ambition, husband and father as forces pulling in different directions daily, and says the imbalance shows up before the explanation does. Learning to read it is what lets you catch the drift early rather than at the end of a year.

**The work is mechanical: whiteboard, ask who am I, peel the layers, name the gap, close it.** Sanders describes carving out time, writing the answer down, then testing it against what he actually enjoys and whether his life contains any of it. When the answer on being present as a parent came back no, the fix was a block: two hours after work that are nothing but family time.

**Becoming more yourself will cost you relationships, and you should plan to recondition the rest.** Sanders points out that your network was partly built around the templated version of you, so the shift is going to read to some people as a change of character. Drost is blunter — sometimes you have to let go of a long-standing friendship to make room for the person you are becoming.

**A written values framework shortens decisions and makes them survivable.** Once the frame is in place, Sanders says choices get faster and easier to live with, because an option that does not infringe on your values needs no further argument. Drost's version is ice cream: the framework is what makes the answer automatic instead of a fresh negotiation every night.

## Try this

- Put ninety minutes on next week's calendar with nothing on the agenda but the question "who am I," and write the answer somewhere you will see it again. Book the second session before you finish the first, since Sanders' whole point is that one sitting will not do it.
- Take the gap you already know about — the hour you are not giving your kids, the reading you keep deferring — and convert it into a recurring calendar block with a start time. Sanders blacks out two hours after work; pick your own number and defend it.
- Read your written values before you make the decision that has been sitting in your inbox for a week. If the option does not conflict with any of them, make the call today and stop paying the deliberation cost.
