## Why this episode

Your firm measures you on two or three numbers, and none of them tell you which of your habits produced this year's work. Doug LaLone rebuilt the problem as a factory process: eight personal metrics a month, colored red, yellow, or green, chained backward from the number he and his wife need to retire. If you want a measurement system rather than a resolution, this is the most operationally specific episode in the archive.

## Guest

Doug LaLone is a partner and patent attorney at Fishman Stewart, a specialty firm dedicated to intellectual property law, and he has been practicing patent law for nearly thirty years in Metro Detroit. He is a Purdue engineering graduate who learned statistical process control in a foundry, and he built this system with two people: his personal coach, and a friend who studied under W. Edwards Deming for six years and installed measurement systems at Chrysler and Toyota. He is explicit that he is not a coach himself. Find him on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/douglalone/).

## Key ideas

**Billable hours, dollars in the door, and realization are too coarse to manage a career with.** LaLone points out these are close to the only things a law firm tracks about a partner, and none of them describe the activity that generated the work. Personal measurement has to be more granular than firm measurement, because you are the only one who can act on it.

**Build the scorecard backward from the retirement number, because retirement is a number you achieve rather than an age you reach.** LaLone starts with what he and his wife need, works back to the annual 1099 figure, and only then asks what in the practice produces it. The income target is metric eight, not metric one.

**The three metrics you fully control involve no clients at all.** Two thought-leadership appearances a month, one new trusted advisor with an actual referral extracted, and five new targets identified. Everything downstream — retained clients, fees collected, hours billed, personal income — is a byproduct he watches rather than an action he takes.

**Diagnostic work replaces the pitch.** LaLone's team builds an IP snapshot mapping the intellectual property across every portfolio company of a target private equity firm, then opens the call by telling the managing director that three of his five companies are red. There is no capabilities deck and no rate sheet in that conversation.

**Alex pushes back on measuring what you cannot steer, and the disagreement is worth sitting with.** His argument is that a missed client target on the twenty-fourth of the month gives you nothing to do about it, while a missed webinar target does. LaLone keeps the outcome numbers on the sheet anyway.

**An all-green year means the goals were soft.** LaLone rebuilds his metrics annually and asks whether he was tough enough on himself, and he holds that a new associate and a thirty-year partner should not be tracking the same things. He is also blunt that doing this alone fails, because you talk yourself out of it.

## Try this

- Pick one monthly metric — not weekly — and start tracking it this week. Monthly survives the unpredictable weeks that kill weekly targets in professional services.
- On the first Monday of next month, put a red, yellow, or green dot next to it for the month just ended, and show the sheet to someone who will ask why the red is red.
- Build one diagnostic document for a single target account this month: what they own, what is exposed, what is unmanaged. Send it with no capabilities pitch attached.
