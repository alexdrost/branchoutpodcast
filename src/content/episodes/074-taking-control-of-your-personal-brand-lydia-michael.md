## Why this episode

You wrote down what you want to be known for and never checked whether anyone else sees you that way. Lydia Michael runs the exercise that closes the gap: you list your attributes, your network lists theirs, and only the overlap is real. If you have ever put a value like authenticity on a slide and then changed nothing about how you operate, this episode names what that costs you.

## Guest

Lydia Michael is founder of Blended Collective, a multicultural marketing and brand consultancy based in Detroit and working nationally and internationally. The firm helps organizations reach and engage diverse audiences and infuse cultural elements into their marketing. She has run brand and marketing programs for small businesses through the Michigan SBDC and the Great Lakes Women's Business Council, and speaks on brand love at the Digital Summit series. Find her on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/lydiamichael/).

## Key ideas

**Brand identity is what you claim; brand image is what your audience holds — and only the overlap is your brand.** Michael's test for the second one is simple: when you are not in the room, what do people say about you? Branding succeeds when the two align, and most rebrands are an admission that they had drifted apart. She borrows the absolute-versus-relative-truth distinction to make the point: outside branding she believes in an objective truth, but inside it only consumer perception operates, which is why brand work done in a vacuum returns the wrong answer.

**Validate your attributes against your network before you build on them.** Write 5 to 10 keywords, then ask clients, colleagues, family and friends the same question, and keep only what appears on both lists. The overlap is where a strong brand can actually be built; the rest goes aside.

**Pick a small number of pillars, because living one costs real resources.** Blended Collective chose authenticity, culture and diversity. To back the first, the firm banned stock images outright and photographs its own events instead — Michael's point being that a pillar you do not operationalize is a checkbox.

**Claiming a value you have not done the work on does more harm than staying quiet.** Diversity became a keyword because there is a return on being associated with it, and customers detect the gap. Her examples are the company that announces its commitment and shows a board of twenty white men, and the ten-year employee who publicly says it was never true.

**Brand love is built by evoking emotion, not by promoting the product.** Humanize the brand, create proximity, personalize the experience, tell stories — and take the senses seriously. Michael points to the scent Abercrombie and Fitch attached to its stores and the scent branding she saw throughout Dubai retail.

**You are a walking billboard whether or not you mean to be.** Write about one topic for a month, she says, and most of your network will associate you with it. The same mechanism that makes people known for things they never chose is the one you can point deliberately.

## Try this

- Take 30 minutes this week and write 5 to 10 words you believe define your brand. Then ask ten people — clients, colleagues, family — what they think of when they hear your name. Circle the words on both lists; those are the ones worth building on.
- Pick your top pillar and audit last month's output against it: posts, proposals, decks, images. Name one concrete practice you will change to make the pillar visible, the way banning stock images made authenticity visible for Michael's firm.
- Before you write your next piece of messaging, answer "if my brand were a person" on paper — how it talks, what it looks like, how it treats people — and write from that.
