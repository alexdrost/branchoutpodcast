## Why this episode

You are losing deals you thought were going well, and you cannot point to the moment it turned. Robert Meyers argues the moment is almost always a small expectation you set and did not meet — a timeline, a document, a callback — and that the fix is mechanical rather than personal. If you have been told business development is a relationship skill you either have or you don't, this is the counterargument: it is a process you can write down, teach and measure.

## Guest

Robert Meyers is President and Managing Member of Republic Business Credit, a non-bank lender. He started in the UK and moved into business development in the middle of a recession, and now trains the firm's business development officers on a broken-out qualification process. Under that approach, referral business at Republic went from roughly 10% three or four years earlier to closer to 40%. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/robmeyers2/).

## Key ideas

**Activity volume guarantees nothing; the activity you choose and the loop you run afterward are the only things you control.** Meyers borrows the leadership distinction — managers do things right, leaders do the right things — and applies it to a pipeline. He wants a business development officer to name the goal of a call before walking in, then spend a minute or two scoring themselves against it before dialing the next one, because that loop is what eventually moves conversion ratios.

**The transaction is a buyer-seller dance of alternating obligations, and your job is to say both sides' steps out loud in plain language.** I do something, you do something, I do something. Meyers' point is that the dance only works if the choreography is stated without jargon, including which steps run in sequence, which run concurrently, and roughly how long each one takes on average.

**A long timeline costs you nothing. A missed one costs you the relationship.** Promise 24 hours and deliver in 72 and you have tripled your stated time and broken trust; promise 96 and deliver in 72 and the client thinks you are excellent. Meyers is blunt that the delivery speed is not the variable — the accuracy of what you said is.

**Apologizing before the client asks is a different act from apologizing after being caught.** Once someone has emailed asking where their document is, the apology is compliance, not ownership. Meyers says the proactive version buys you something durable and the reactive version is gone by the next day.

**Meyers trains his team to interrupt their own sale and send prospects to a bank first.** Republic is more expensive than a bank, so the qualifying question is whether the prospect has talked to one; if the answer is no, the process pauses for 30 to 60 days and the team hands over names. He is unembarrassed about being plan B, and treats the lost deals as the price of being the obvious call later.

**Your strongest references are clients who were once unhappy and whose problem you fixed.** Unbroken praise reads as staged to a prospect. Meyers keeps a standing list of 10 to 15 reference clients and put an incentive plan behind staff actually asking for referrals — the machinery, again, rather than hoping good service converts itself.

## Try this

- Write your sales process as named steps with an average duration next to each, and mark which run in sequence and which run concurrently. Read that list to your next prospect on the first call, and open with the timing and urgency question before you describe anything you sell.
- On your next three qualification calls, ask the disqualifying question — Meyers' version is "have you talked to a bank yet?" — and be willing to pause for 30 to 60 days and hand over competitor names. Track what comes back in six months.
- Buy a notebook and, after your next sales call, write three lines: what, so what, what next. Go back to that entry a week later and see whether the takeaway held.
