## Why this episode

Your firm keeps starting things it does not finish, and everyone is being told to be more curious about all of it. Gary Thomson, who spent 34 years in public accounting before turning to consulting, argues the problem is not a shortage of curiosity but the absence of any boundary around it. If you want a way to decide what to be curious about, and to get real freedom to chase it once you have decided, this is the episode.

## Guest

Gary Thomson spent 34 years in public accounting. He walked into Arthur Andersen in June 1985, later started and grew his own firm through a run of M&A activity, and ended his firm career at DHG, now part of FORVIS. He retired from practice in 2019, has served on AICPA Council, and now consults to CPA and professional services firms on strategic planning, governance structures, and partner-level leadership development through Thomson Consulting. Find him on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/gary-thomson-a9158943/).

## Key ideas

**Curiosity has a cost, and firms that chase everything pay it in initiative fatigue.** Thomson consults with firms pursuing every new object in the market and describes their people as dizzy and worn out. He names the symptom initiative fatigue and treats it as a strategy failure, not a lack of enthusiasm.

**A firm does not need everyone curious about everything.** What it needs is each person's curiosity pointed at the place where their highest and best use meets what the firm is actually trying to do. Thomson's example is blunt: rebuilding an audit methodology excites almost nobody, but if you are good at it, that is where your exploration belongs.

**Accountability grants exploratory freedom rather than removing it.** Thomson cites a study finding that the most entrepreneurial organizations carry the highest degree of accountability, and says it reversed his own assumption. Once parameters, budget, and a deadline are agreed, you stop going back for permission every time you have an idea.

**Individual goals are meaningless until firm strategy and service-line strategy exist in writing.** He describes a three-level cascade: firm, then department or service line, then you. Without the first two levels, nobody is telling you yes and nobody is telling you no, so your plan cannot be wrong and cannot be right.

**You propose your own goals and expect your leader to edit them.** Thomson's version of goal setting is neither decreed from above nor declared from below. You bring a list built off the cascade, some of it gets struck, something you did not think of gets added, and the accountability that follows is something you agreed to.

**Leaders kill ideas with reactions, not refusals.** A huff, a sigh, a glance down at the phone: nothing was rejected, but the energy behind the idea is gone. Thomson pairs this with a sunk-cost argument, describing a firm that wrote off roughly $150,000 of December 2019 conference-room video equipment rather than let the spend dictate its remote-work decision three months later.

## Try this

- Before your next goal-setting meeting, ask for the firm's strategy and your service line's strategy in writing. If neither exists on paper, that request is the more useful conversation.
- Draft your own goal list and bring it in as a proposal rather than a finished document. Two struck and one added is the outcome Thomson is describing, and it is the point of the exercise.
- Take one thing you have been meaning to explore and get three numbers attached to it this week: what you can spend, what you are allowed to decide alone, and the date you report back. Thomson's own example is 60 days to bring technology recommendations.
