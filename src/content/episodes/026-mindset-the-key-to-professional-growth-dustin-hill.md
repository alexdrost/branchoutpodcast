## Why this episode

You know the soft skills are what carry you, and you cannot prove that a single hour spent on them returned anything, so the hours quietly go back to work that shows. Dustin Hill and Alex Drost sit inside that problem rather than talking around it, to the point where Drost concedes on air that he cannot demonstrate results for the business he built on it. What you get instead of proof is a way to decide what deserves protected time.

## Guest

Dustin Hill is a director with FINNEA Group, a middle-market investment banking firm based in Birmingham, Michigan, where he works with owners selling their businesses. His stated why comes from home: a father who kept an unflappable optimism through businesses that never materialized, and a mother whose employer folded for lack of a succession plan. He runs his week off three written items each morning and a Sunday review, with notifications turned off on every device. Find him at [linkedin.com/in/dustinhillmba](https://www.linkedin.com/in/dustinhillmba/).

## Key ideas

**Professionals default to hard skills because hard skills can be counted.** You either can build the model or you cannot. Soft-skill investment resists measurement entirely, which is why people substitute proxies like business cards collected and hands shaken, and why those proxies tell you nothing.

**Hill defines growth as your capabilities converging on your why, which means without a why you cannot tell whether growth happened.** There is no end state and no instant gratification, only iteration. The why is what replaces the missing metric, and it is different for every person.

**You overestimate a week and underestimate a year, and that asymmetry is why soft-skill progress feels invisible.** You reach Friday having finished Monday's list. You reach December surprised at how much moved. Neither feeling is available in the moment when you are deciding whether to spend two hours on something unquantifiable.

**Inaction is comfortable precisely because you cannot tell whether the effort is the right effort.** Fear of doing it wrong produces doing nothing, which is strictly worse. Hill's answer is to dive in and accept that the direction gets corrected on the way.

**Most growth in a services business happens outside business hours, and that is not an argument for hundred-hour weeks.** The workday gets consumed by execution, so the initiative has to come from somewhere else. Hill is explicit that this means reading, the gym, journaling and reflection rather than more billable work.

**Put a number on everything in your urgent bucket and most of it collapses.** Four hours spent chasing a fifty-dollar saving on car insurance pays you $12.50 an hour. Hill assigns dollars or a zero-to-ten score to each item, then compares it against what the important-but-not-urgent column could return.

## Try this

- Turn off email and text notifications on your phone and your desktop today, then block two hours later this week where you do not open the inbox at all. Tell the one person who genuinely might need you.
- On Sunday, write what you meant to accomplish last week beside what you actually finished, and build next week's list from the gap rather than from optimism. Three items each morning; leave the forty-item list on the computer.
- Take everything in your urgent bucket and score each item in dollars or zero to ten. Cut or defer anything that scores below the two hours you just blocked, and put one important-but-not-urgent item into that block.
