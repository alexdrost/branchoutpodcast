## Why this episode

If your work pays off two years from now, you are being asked to justify it every quarter, and the numbers you can produce say almost nothing about whether you are doing it well. Polly Mack and Heather Madland run business development inside private equity firms, and both argue the job is at least as much about credibility with your own partners as with the market — and that counting inbound deals is a way of measuring nothing. This is also for the analyst or associate who assumes business development is somebody else's function.

## Guest

Polly Mack is Director of Business Development for Gryphon Investors, a thesis-driven firm whose sectors keep moving, which means her job includes knowing what the firm wants this quarter well enough to say it accurately in the market. She had recently changed firms when this was recorded, and says plainly that the network and reputation she had built are what she was hired for. Heather Madland is a partner and Head of Business Development with Huron Capital, where she runs the firm's business development training for associates and hired a presence coach when she recognized that what got her there would not get her further. They are on LinkedIn: [Polly Mack](https://www.linkedin.com/in/pollymack/), [Heather Madland](https://www.linkedin.com/in/heathermadland/).

## Key ideas

**Business development is an internal job before it is an external one.** Madland's framing: you need enough credibility inside the firm that partners believe you are not wasting their time, and enough outside that a prospect believes you actually speak for the firm. Neither is achievable through branding alone.

**Deal volume is not success.** Mack refuses to count more teasers as evidence she is doing her job, because volume says nothing about quality and nothing about whether the firm got an edge. Alex Drost's version is to set goals on the inputs you control and treat outcomes as a byproduct you watch rather than a target you chase.

**Everyone in the firm is doing business development, including the associate who never leaves the spreadsheet.** They are the ones negotiating NDAs, asking early questions and turning deals down, which makes them the market's first impression of the firm. Drost, a former sell-side banker, says he can still name the three firms whose NDA behavior cost them looks at deals.

**How you pass on a deal is a discipline with its own page in the training deck.** Madland teaches it deliberately: not "we're not interested" after taking the book, but a thoughtful why plus what you are actually looking for. Mack's test is simpler — someone gave you an hour of their time, so answer them.

**The skill set runs past communication into analytics, creativity and strategy.** Madland credits the shift in her own career to putting on a visionary mindset and bringing data and words to the firm's long-term business development strategy. Mack adds influence: much of the job is persuading colleagues to do things that are not in their comfort zone, without it ever feeling like selling.

**Your network and your reputation are portable, so behave as if they are the asset.** Both treat business development as a career rather than a role. Conduct on the road, at a conference, in a negotiation follows you between employers, and Mack's proprietary deal came from a former portfolio company CFO who already knew how the firm treated its partners.

## Try this

- The next time you close a deal or finish a large project, go to three or four colleagues and ask "how did I do?" rather than waiting for the annual review to tell you.
- Call two bankers or referral sources from a recent process, won or lost, and ask both how the firm performed and what they wish you personally had done differently in dealing with them.
- Take the next deal or opportunity you decline and write the response properly: why it is not a fit, and what you are looking for instead. Two minutes, and it is the version of your firm they will remember.
