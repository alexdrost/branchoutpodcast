## Why this episode

You screen candidates on the school and the GPA because those are the numbers that compare cleanly, and you keep hiring the same person. Charmone Adams was the youngest of seven and the first in his family to finish college, and he can tell you exactly what that costs a candidate — not intelligence, but knowledge of the preparation rules nobody writes down. He also argues that the drive the gap produces is the trait you cannot teach and the one your screens are built to miss.

## Guest

Charmone Adams is a senior manager at Grant Thornton, focused on advisory services to financial institutions. He grew up in Cleveland in a blue-collar family as the youngest of seven, and was the first of them to complete both a bachelor's and a master's degree. He interviews candidates for the firm and works with clients on their own diversity requirements. Find him on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/charmone-adams-cpa-01513a11/).

## Key ideas

**The first-generation disadvantage is procedural, not intellectual.** Adams did not know the ACT required writing preparation, so he sat it the day after his 18th birthday having gone out the night before. What he lacked was the college-prep calendar other families hand down — the practice tests taken in ninth grade, the visits, the strategy for a top score.

**Feeling permanently behind produced the habit that made his career.** He describes reading publications and listening to podcasts constantly as playing catch-up that never ended. The deficit turned into an appetite for learning that outlasted the thing it was compensating for.

**Recruiting from the same schools guarantees the same output.** Adams is blunt that a cookie-cutter screen built on target schools will keep returning target-school results. His claim is about work product, not optics: the quality of what a team delivers improves specifically when the perspectives in it do not match.

**Code switching began as survival, and Adams argues it is now an act of respect.** He traces it to Black Americans boxed out of jobs and mortgages during the Civil Rights era, and says it remains essential to navigating corporate America. His own modern example is choosing jazz over rap in a board meeting — a courtesy, in his framing, rather than a concession. Alex pushes back on the softer version, arguing nobody should feel they must present a specific way to be let into the circle.

**Client pressure, not conscience, is what actually moved firms.** Adams points to the New York Stock Exchange board diversity requirements and the ESG wave: clients now ask about DEI in proposals, look at the consulting pool, and look through to vendor lists. His conclusion is that firms which stay traditional will not be sustainable.

**What he screens for is authenticity about the candidate's own path.** He claims he can read a candidate within seconds of conversation, and what he is reading is whether they own their journey rather than apologize for it. The teacher moving into consulting and the person who started college at 21 both bring years that count as applicable experience.
