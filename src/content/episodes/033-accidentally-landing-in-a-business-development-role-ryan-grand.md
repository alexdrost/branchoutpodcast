## Why this episode

You are the technical one. You get nothing out of working a room of 200 people, and somewhere along the way you concluded that rules you out of business development. Ryan Grand took the BD seat at a private equity firm as a waypoint to a transaction role, was offered the transfer three years later, and turned it down. What he describes is a volume-and-record-keeping job that rewards the person who prefers one conversation at a time — and he is unusually specific about the machinery behind it.

## Guest

Ryan Grand is a principal with HKW, an Indianapolis-based middle-market private equity firm with a 100-plus-year history. He spent about four and a half years as a municipal bond underwriter at Mesirow and Jefferies, went to business school to change direction, interned at HKW on the transaction side, and took the only seat that was open, which was in business development. Six years in, he is still there. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ryangrand/).

## Key ideas

**Sourcing is a volume system before it is a relationship skill.** What HKW handed Grand on day one was not a technique but a promise: roughly 40 to 45 trips and 500 in-person meetings a year, and the results will follow. That is what he did for his first two years, and he credits the fact that a system already existed for making an unfamiliar job survivable.

**The hard part of the switch was learning to go shallow across many deals instead of deep on one.** In transaction work Grand was the person who dug in, decided what was broken, and killed it or carried it. In sourcing, each deal is one of many, the job is triage, and the measure is whether the transaction team likes what lands in the pipeline.

**Expecting to be the first call on every fitting deal is conceited.** With roughly 4,500 private equity groups in the market, Grand's target is to be reliably in anyone's top 20. He gets there by never getting angry when a banker skips him and by saying the same collaborative thing every time — internally they call it being persistent but not to the point of annoying.

**The decision to sign an NDA is where a sourcing professional's credibility is spent.** Grand screens hard before he signs, because taking a look at a deal his team will obviously reject burns the banker's time and makes HKW look like it is kicking tires. He thinks most bankers prefer the fast, specific no, and he would rather be the person who gives it.

**The job rewards someone who does not enjoy the conference floor.** Grand says plainly that he is not gregarious in large rooms and gets no energy from ping-ponging between 200 people. His argument is that private equity sourcing is actually built out of small one-on-one conversations repeated over years, which makes the quiet operator well suited rather than disadvantaged.

**The system is what buys back the time to be human.** HKW keeps a spreadsheet of the top 65 US and Canadian markets recording last contact, target touches per year, actual touches, and who is up next, reallocated every quarter. Drost takes on the objection directly: people who call a CRM inauthentic have it backwards, because the recall it gives you is what makes the next conversation a real one.

## Try this

- Build the coverage sheet this week. List the markets or referral sources your pipeline actually depends on, the date you last spoke to each, and how many times a year you intend to. Then put the three that have gone quietest into next week's calendar.
- Write the note immediately after your next conversation, before you open anything else. Grand admits he leans on his memory more than he should; Drost's argument is that the act of writing it is what surfaces the introduction or follow-up you would otherwise never make.
- Take the last meeting of the next conference — the one you were going to skip for an earlier flight. Grand says roughly half the deals they have gotten came out of meeting number 35.
