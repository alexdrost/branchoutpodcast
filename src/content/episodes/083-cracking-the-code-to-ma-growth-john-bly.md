## Why this episode

You run a small professional firm, growth is organic and slow, and buying another practice feels like something only bigger firms do. John Bly did fifteen acquisitions in fifteen years starting from his house at 25, and his account is specific about what actually broke deals — not valuation, but mismatched expectations he could surface in the first two hours. It is also useful if you are on the other side, sizing up an approach you assume is a waste of your time.

## Guest

John Bly is South Atlantic Regional Leader for Aprio, a top 35 CPA firm headquartered in Atlanta, where he is a regional managing partner and sits on the board. He started his career at PwC, then founded a CPA firm in Charlotte out of his house in 2004 at 25 with his wife, also a CPA. The firm — LBA Haynes Strand — went from about $1.5 million to $7.5 million between 2009 and 2014 and merged into Aprio in 2019. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/johnblycpa/).

## Key ideas

**Big 4 partnership looks like the pinnacle from outside and functions like employment from inside.** Bly wanted to be a CPA from age ten and assumed partner was the end state. What he found was people with a K-1 and limited decision-making autonomy, running a practice within a practice.

**On a professional services acquisition, diligence the work product, not just the financials.** Bly's first four deals were clean. On the fifth, he had done the usual review of integration, tech, people and financials — and spent the following busy season telling inherited clients that positions their prior CPA had taken were plainly wrong. His shorthand for the lesson: a CPA does not equal a CPA.

**Pruning clients and service lines accelerated growth rather than slowing it.** Between 2006 and 2009 the firm sold three pockets of clients and its payroll business to ADP, Paychex and PayChoice, and still posted revenue growth in those years. Bly's reasoning is capacity: you cannot grow in the areas you care about while defending revenue you do not.

**Whether the counterparty is staying or leaving should change the entire diligence question.** If they exit in twelve months, the work is client and staff transition. If they stay five years and take equity, Bly says he cares much less about the clients and much more about whether this person adapts to change, leads rather than does, and can answer to someone else.

**Give up the marginal economics, because the accounting M&A market is small and the exiting partner refers your next deal.** Bly's rule is that if $10,000 matters a lot to the other side, they can have it. Partners he bought out came back a year or two later with referrals — a return he describes as unexpected.

**Take the two-hour meeting even with no intent to transact.** The Aprio merger traces back to a broker call seven or eight years earlier that Bly could easily have declined. What he sees blocking other owners is ego, identity fused to being the owner of X, and "I don't have time" — which Alex Drost reframes as a statement about priority, not capacity.

## Try this

- In the first two hours of any acquisition conversation, ask the other side outright what matters most — the people, the clients, staying five years, getting out in twelve months — and use the answer to pass fast on bad fits. When the demands do not reconcile, ask them to show the math on paper rather than arguing it.
- Add work-product and tax-position review to your buy-side diligence checklist alongside financial, technology and people review. Bly changed his process only after a busy season of delivering bad news to clients he had just bought.
- Take the next unsolicited call from a broker or a peer firm and give it two hours, with no intent to transact and no agenda. Say so up front, the way Bly did.
