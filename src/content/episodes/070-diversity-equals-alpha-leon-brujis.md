## Why this episode

If you have ever sat through a diversity discussion that felt like compliance, Leon Brujis is arguing that the framing is the reason. He makes the case entirely in investment terms — where returns come from, why a homogeneous team cannot see what the market has missed — and he tells you where his argument stops applying. Useful whether you run a firm's hiring or just want a version of this conversation you can take to an investment committee.

## Guest

Leon Brujis is a partner at Palladium Equity Partners, a middle-market private equity firm in New York, and serves on the firm's Investment and Management Committee. He was born in Mexico, raised in Argentina, and his parents are from Peru; he began on the Fannie Mae trading floor, spent time at Lehman Brothers, and joined Palladium in 2007. He sits on the board of The New America Alliance. Find him at [linkedin.com/in/lbrujis](https://www.linkedin.com/in/lbrujis/).

## Key ideas

**Brujis rejects the moral framing on purpose, and says so in his first answer.** His view is that treating diversity as the politically correct thing to do turns it into a box-ticking exercise, and that box-ticking is precisely what leads to diverse voices not being properly included. The substitute framing is that diversity equals alpha.

**Alpha lives in judgment, and judgment requires people who did not follow the pack.** Investing is gathering data and then making a bet; the return attributable to the manager rather than the market comes from seeing what others do not see. If your team went to the same schools and grew up in the same places, Brujis argues, you are structurally positioned to reach the average.

**Diversity is not universally optimal, and he volunteers the counterexample himself.** In his Olympic relay analogy, if you could clone Usain Bolt three times you would not want a diverse team, because there is one right answer and he is it. The argument holds for complex judgment and problem-solving, not for every task.

**He treats the claim as measurable and corrects the host for saying otherwise.** When Alex suggests the effect cannot really be tracked, Brujis pushes back: there are studies showing diverse boards improve performance at publicly traded companies, and that private equity managers with diverse organizations outperform the average. He also cites the industry gap — 1.3% of a $70 trillion asset management industry managed by women or diverse managers.

**Incrementalism is the failure mode, so set targets you expect to miss.** Brujis's three-part plan is intent, then metrics and transparency, then bold action. He is blunt about the arithmetic: a firm of 1,000 that hires 10 diverse people has changed nothing. Reaching critical mass is what makes the effect visible, which is why he prefers aspirational targets to achievable ones.

**Nobody who qualifies wants a handout, so the discipline is searching harder rather than lowering the bar.** Brujis says he would not want a job offered because of how he looks; he wants it offered on his track record. The work he is asking for is looking outside the concentric circles you are comfortable in, and accepting that it is slow and difficult.

## Try this

- Write down the last 20 people you had a substantive professional conversation with and mark each one's school, home region and first employer. Brujis's claim is that you will be surprised how homogeneous the list is; the exercise takes twenty minutes and either confirms it or does not.
- Set one hiring or sourcing target for the year that you publish internally and genuinely expect to miss, and pair it with the metric you will report against. His argument is that getting halfway to an exceptional number beats hitting an incremental one.
- Contact one existing pipeline organization instead of building sourcing yourself. Brujis names SEO, the National Association of Investment Companies and The New America Alliance as places his firm works with.
