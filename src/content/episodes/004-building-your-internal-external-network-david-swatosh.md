## Why this episode

You were told to build a network, and you may have spent years building the wrong one. David Swatosh moved from a mid-size regional accounting firm to BDO and found that his attorneys, bankers and controllers were all sized for a smaller client than his new firm sold to, while roughly 80 percent of his work arrived through partners sitting inside his own building. If your calendar is full of external events that never convert, this episode is about where to put those hours instead.

## Guest

David Swatosh is a senior manager with BDO, specializing in the R&D tax credit and, at the time of recording, the newly created employee retention credit. He calls himself an expert in sections 41 and 174 of the Internal Revenue Code and is candid that fifteen years into the profession he was still doing the networking he thought accountants were supposed to do. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/dswatosh/).

## Key ideas

**Map your revenue before you plan your networking.** Swatosh sorted his sources into tiers — BDO's internal partners and directors first, the firm's alliance network of smaller CPA firms second, outside contacts after that — and aimed his time accordingly. He describes the alternative as throwing darts at a wall and hoping one sticks.

**For a technical specialist, the internal network is the market.** Around 80 percent of his projects come from clients BDO already serves, which makes partners, directors and senior managers the referral market that matters. Winning them over is a trust question: they need to believe he will take care of their client.

**Referral sources cannot sell what they cannot see, so educating them is the work.** The tax code is gray, and that gray area is where his expertise lives. Before a partner can spot the opportunity, someone has to teach them what the opportunity looks like.

**Send ammunition, not asks.** A thought leadership piece, a draft email a partner can forward, or three questions to raise on the next client call all beat a request for an introduction. When a rule changes, Swatosh takes it to the firm's weekly and monthly business development meetings so the whole office is current.

**A lateral move can invalidate a network without changing a single skill.** Nothing about his technical work changed when he moved from regional to national; the target client moved from the lower middle market to the upper middle market, and his relationships no longer pointed at the buyers.

**Networking style is a fit question, and forcing the wrong one is expensive.** Swatosh worked the Detroit Economic Club, MICPA and ACG because that is what accountants do, and only recently concluded he is an introvert for whom none of it was ever going to work. Drost, an extrovert who enjoys those rooms, agrees the norm is not the point.

## Try this

- Write down where your last ten engagements actually originated — internal partner, alliance firm, outside referral, direct inbound — then put that list next to last month's calendar and see whether the two match.
- Build the three-tier prospect list Swatosh keeps: high priority, in between, long shot. Name the person who owns each relationship, and send one of those owners a note this week offering client-service help on a specific account.
- Take the next change in your specialty and turn it into a draft email your partners can forward to clients without editing a word. Ask for ten minutes on the agenda of the next firm business development meeting to walk through it.
