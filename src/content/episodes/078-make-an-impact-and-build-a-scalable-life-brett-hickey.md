## Why this episode

You are busy, you feel behind, and your default when it gets worse is to push harder. Brett Hickey argues the default is backwards, and that a surprising share of your output is set by things you treat as optional — when you sleep, how far you live from the office, which hour you put your hardest thinking in. He also makes the case that the career decisions worth making look wrong on a one-year view and obvious on a ten-year one.

## Guest

Brett Hickey is founder and CEO of Star Mountain Capital, a specialized investment firm working exclusively in the US lower middle market, with businesses generally between 15 million and 250 million dollars in annual revenue. He grew up in a town of about 10,000 people in northwestern Canada, skated on the national speed skating team and set a national record, and worked oil drilling rigs after high school to pay for college. He went into investment banking at Citigroup before starting his first lower middle market investment firm in 2004. Find him on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/bhickey/).

## Key ideas

**Write a researched business plan for your own life before you start running at it.** Hickey says to study it, stress test it, and work from real data rather than your own assumptions and biases. His observation is that people skip this step because planning feels like a delay when you are eager, and then spend years correcting.

**Charging harder is the wrong answer to overwhelm.** The instinct is to scramble; Hickey's prescription is to step back, cut the inputs, and do one thing well before moving to the next. It feels slower and produces more. He is candid that even a CEO's to-do list never finishes, which makes prioritizing the highest-impact item the only real move.

**Sleep, nutrition and fitness are a fiduciary matter when you manage other people's capital.** Hickey abandoned the "sleeping is for wimps" position he once held, because high-quality thought is the job. The same logic runs down to the specifics: he does yoga on Monday mornings so that he arrives at the Monday staff meeting calibrated rather than agitated.

**Engineer friction out of the physical layout of your life.** He lived within walking distance of the office as a junior banker, replicated the Harvard OPM campus experience where eating, exercising and working all sat in one place, and opened a Tampa Bay office so people could avoid a New York commute. He also picks green tea over coffee to flatten the highs and lows.

**A genuinely differentiated product buys you headwinds, and the real work is engineering the transfer of trust.** Human nature is to take comfort in what already exists, which by definition is not the innovative thing, so Star Mountain found its early traction with investors who had independent thought and real underwriting capability. Hickey borrows from film marketing — this movie is A meets B — to argue that relatability is how you get someone to grasp a thing they have not seen, and that the harder half is giving people confidence you will deliver what you say.

**Judge a career on the slope of the ten-year curve, not the starting point.** Hickey drew no salary for 11 years in order to invest in building Star Mountain. His advice is to graph two paths ten years out and decide on the second number, because pennywise, pound-foolish decisions about near-term money are common and costly.

## Try this

- Open next week's calendar and move your single highest-impact task into the hour when your energy peaks and you are least required to be responsive. Move one recurring catch-up to the end of the day, where sharpness is not needed.
- List the three biggest sources of friction in your week — the commute, the meal decisions, the errand you resent — and eliminate or offload one of them this month.
- For the choice in front of you, sketch both options as curves: where each starts and where each is in ten years. Decide on the ten-year point and write down what the first year costs you.
