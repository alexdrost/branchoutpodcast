## Why this episode

Your firm's work is genuinely interchangeable with the firm down the street, and pretending otherwise in a pitch is not working. Emily Ackerman starts from that admission rather than arguing around it, and rebuilds business development on the one variable competitors cannot copy. If you are in a market where clients only switch firms after a death, a retirement or a screw-up, this is the mechanics of staying top of mind until one of those happens.

## Guest

Emily Ackerman is a business development executive with Carr, Riggs & Ingram, a top 25 CPA firm, working the Dallas–Fort Worth market. She is the only person in that role in her market without a CPA or finance background, which she treats as the reason she was hired. She spent the first part of her ten-year business development career at CBS Radio before moving into public accounting. Find her at [linkedin.com/in/emilyackermandfw](https://www.linkedin.com/in/emilyackermandfw/).

## Key ideas

**Every firm of the same size does the same work, so the differentiator has to be the person and the story.** Ackerman says this plainly rather than treating it as a weakness to manage: audit is audit, the technical claim is unavailable, and what is left is the firm's history and how memorable its representative is. Her firm's pitch is a 75-year story and a Goldilocks position, not a capability claim.

**Cold outreach is the wrong instrument in a business where nobody switches.** Clients leave a CPA relationship on a death, a retirement, a move or a major failure. That makes the job being remembered at the moment one of those things happens, not generating a response today.

**Sales is dating, and one coffee is enough to know.** Ackerman treats the first meeting as a fit assessment running in both directions and has told prospective clients outright that they are not a fit. She has a companion rule for the people she does rate: if she cannot bring them in as a client, she introduces them to people they need to know.

**Deliberately fish where nobody else is fishing.** At CBS Radio she sold to men 35 and up while every competitor chased women 25 to 54. At CRI she works manufacturing in Garland, Texas, where one other firm shows up, instead of the chambers everybody joins. She describes it as being the big fish in a not-so-big pond.

**Community involvement only pays when the passion is real.** Ackerman skips the associations her competitors all belong to and gives her volunteer time to special needs advocacy, because she has a special needs brother she expects to care for. Forced involvement consumes the same hours and returns nothing.

**Ration the format of a meeting, not the invitation.** She will meet almost anyone, including the big banks she has no realistic shot at, because people change employers and referrals arrive from strange places. What she will not do is spend two hours in a car for a first impression that a video call handles.

## Try this

- List the industry groups and chambers you belong to, then find the one your three closest competitors all attend and drop it. Replace it with a geography or industry in your market that nobody is covering, and go to its next event.
- Replace your next two coffee meetings with something that is not coffee — a walk, a workout, getting something done you both need done anyway. Ackerman's test is whether the other person loosens up.
- Go through next week's calendar and re-sort every meeting by format before it happens: anything that is a first impression more than 30 minutes away becomes a video call, and the hours you get back go to a longer Friday lunch with someone already in your network.
