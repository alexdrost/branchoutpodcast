## Why this episode

You have been running the numbers on leaving, and every version of the spreadsheet says stay. Ken Marblestone left commercial banking, investment banking and commercial real estate to co-found an acquisition firm at 60, and his argument is that the spreadsheet is missing a column: what staying somewhere that does not fit you costs your health, and what it costs the people who have to work alongside you. He is candid that the mistake he actually made was waiting.

## Guest

Ken Marblestone is co-founder and managing partner at Ten:34 Partners, a private investment firm that buys family and entrepreneur-owned businesses in healthcare and business services. He spent a long career working with family business owners across commercial banking, investment banking and commercial real estate before starting the firm with his partner Justin during COVID. The name comes from the minute before the Wright Brothers' first flight, and from their own path through printing and bicycles to get there. Find him on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ken-marblestone-10289546).

## Key ideas

**Risk sits on both sides of the ledger, and most professionals only price one of them.** Marblestone measures the cost of starting something, and also the cost of staying. At his last large corporation, his honest read was that staying was going to create a health problem for him. Evaluate only the financial side and you will conclude, correctly by that arithmetic and wrongly overall, that you should not move.

**A poor role fit is not a private cost you absorb alone.** If the job does not fit you and you work with other people, Marblestone says, you are degrading their experience too. This is the argument that removes the self-indulgence objection from career change, and it is the one he makes most flatly.

**Getting better at what you are already good at beats remediating what you are not.** He credits StrengthsFinder for the frame and calls it an optimistic view: rather than torturing yourself over your weak spots, find what you are good at and do more of it. Human default runs the other way, which is why the advice needs stating.

**He was "not bad" at corporate politics and stopped trying to improve at it.** Marblestone knew people who were genuinely skilled at bureaucracy and thought they should keep doing it. He also knew he saw consequences too clearly and said what he thought too freely to ever be good at it. His conclusion was to move to work that did not require the skill rather than change his personality.

**Relationship building is not business generation, and nobody can predict where the business comes from.** He has never been a good predictor of who he will get business from. What he has found reliable is genuine interest, which people detect, plus helping when you can. At events, he tells you to break away from the colleagues you arrived with and talk to people you do not know.

**A referral is someone spending their reputation on you, which is why firms that ignore small work never get a second one.** Marblestone worked at companies whose rule was that anything too small to make money on got no time. His objection is not that the math is wrong but that it ruins who he is, and that the referral source never sends another. He also names the introspection method: observe your own day as a third party, quiet the internal dialogue, and write down when you felt you were making a difference.

## Try this

- Take 30 minutes this week with a blank page and free-write what you enjoy and what you are good at, without stopping to judge whether any item belongs there. Date it, put it away, and reread it in two months. This is Marblestone's own call to action, and his addition is: write fast, do not hesitate.
- Keep a one-week log of the specific moments you felt you were connecting with someone or making a difference. Use that list, not your job description, to decide where next quarter's discretionary time goes.
- The next referral you receive, report back to the person who made it before they have to ask — including when nothing came of it. Marblestone's own example of getting this wrong is a call he made and forgot to mention.
