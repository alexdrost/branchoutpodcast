## Why this episode

Your firm keeps sending you to sales training, and the client conversations that actually work look nothing like what the training describes. Lenny Dacanay held the sales view of business development for the first half of a twenty-year career, then rebuilt the role around problem solving - which is why the money now goes to executive education and an executive coach instead. If you have hit the point where telling clients things has stopped working, this is a short argument for what replaces it.

## Guest

Lenny Dacanay works in business development for the middle market at BDO USA; the episode notes that his views are his own rather than the firm's. He is a former business owner and owner-operator, and he has worked with an executive coach for two years, which he describes as changing his professional and personal life. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/lenny-dacanay-63526810/).

## Key ideas

**A true business development executive is a problem solver, not a salesperson.** Dacanay is direct that he used to see it the other way. The reframe is what justifies the spending: if the job is solving problems in a world that has changed, the skills to buy are analytical and human, not closing technique.

**The craft moved from telling to asking.** The goal of a client conversation is to surface root causes and the objectives clients leave unstated. Dacanay says the highest compliment a client can pay him is that a question needs thinking about.

**Honesty has to be gated by empathy and explicit permission.** When a conversation is heading somewhere a client may not be ready to go, he asks permission before going there. It is a softer line than the brutal-candor position other guests on this show take, and he defends it on the grounds that trust takes time.

**A coach is bought for accountability, not just skill.** Dacanay interviewed several and chose the one whose questions peeled his goals back furthest. What he credits her with is honest self-assessment and being held to it - his test being that you cannot lead others if you cannot lead yourself.

**If everything is a priority, nothing is.** He runs two questions through the day as distractions arrive: what is important now, and what is the highest and best use of my time. He is explicit that treating everything as urgent used to mean the important things never got done.

**Calling clients in a crisis to check in is welcomed, not intrusive.** Across more than 150 calls and Zooms in three months, nobody told him they were too busy to talk, and the common response was thanks for checking in. That is the evidence, not the theory.

## Try this

- Before your next three client meetings, write the questions instead of the update. Aim for one question per meeting that makes the other person say they need to think about it.
- The next time you are about to tell a client something they will not want to hear, ask permission out loud first - can we go there - and let their answer set the pace.
- Take the next distraction that lands on you and, before you respond to it, answer two questions in writing: is this actually a priority, and what is the highest and best use of the next hour. Put the loser on a list for later rather than doing it now.
