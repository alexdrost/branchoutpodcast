## Why this episode

You may be trying to hire from groups your firm has never reached and cannot work out why nobody applies. Joshua Berggrun answers that from the candidate's side of the table: what he looked for as a gay law student, what he found on most firm websites, and which recruiting channels do not exist for him at all. If you are looking for a specific first move rather than a statement of values, this is a short episode with concrete ones.

## Guest

Joshua Berggrun is an associate attorney at Calfee, Halter & Griswold in Cleveland, Ohio, where he practices real estate and real estate finance and supports the firm's M&A groups on the real estate components of transactions. Born and raised in Cleveland, he went to law school at Elon University in the south. He speaks here as a member of the LGBT community about how he chose his firm and what he thinks recruiting is missing. Find him at [linkedin.com/in/joshua-berggrun-58144b28](https://www.linkedin.com/in/joshua-berggrun-58144b28/).

## Key ideas

**Berggrun starts from the moral case and treats the business case as additional.** Asked why an inclusive workplace matters, his first sentence is that it is the right thing to do. Different viewpoints, better client service and better hiring decisions follow in his answer as further reasons, not as the argument.

**A committee listed on a firm's website changed where he applied.** He concedes it can be a placeholder. It was still the only visible difference between his firm and most of the others he considered, which had no mention of diversity anywhere on the site or in the interview. Pairing it with allocated resources and Mansfield Rule certification is what makes it more than a placeholder.

**LGBT law students have no recruiting channel of their own.** There is no dedicated career fair and no on-campus interview track, which means that unless you are top of the class, you never get in front of the major firms. Berggrun's ask of firms is to fund and attend the career fairs that would fill that gap.

**A transaction career means coming out repeatedly for the rest of your working life.** He does it daily and expects to keep doing it. His point is what that prospect does to a student who is not yet secure or not yet out: they do not apply. His message to those listeners is that people like him are already in these jobs.

**The people who enter anyway tend to be the strongest hires.** Berggrun's observation is that anyone who joins a profession with no guarantee the workplace will be inclusive already has the self-assurance and drive that makes them a star, and that male-dominated industries would benefit from looking for exactly those candidates.

**The most useful thing a committee does may be the most mundane.** A firm-wide email to 500 people telling them what legislation is being debated in Ohio, or running an event with the LGBT chamber of commerce, reaches colleagues who experience no diversity at home and will never seek that information out on a busy day. Berggrun also thinks it is a good sign, not tokenism, that his firm's committee is run by allies.

## Try this

- Open your firm's website as a candidate would and find out how many clicks it takes to learn whether any inclusion effort exists. If the answer is that it does not appear, that is the gap Berggrun is describing, and fixing it is a web page and a named group of people.
- Ask whoever runs recruiting which career fairs and campus events the firm pays to attend, then add one aimed specifically at LGBT or minority students to next year's budget.
- Send one firm-wide email this quarter naming legislation currently in front of your state legislature that affects colleagues, or co-host one event with a local affinity chamber. Berggrun's argument is that busy professionals will not go looking, so someone has to put it in front of them.
