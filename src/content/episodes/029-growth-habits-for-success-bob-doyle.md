## Why this episode

You know the non-technical side of your career is what actually moves it, and it keeps losing to the work that bills. Bob Doyle protects the hour before he opens his laptop for reading, journaling and one written line of gratitude, and he refuses to let any of it be called soft. If your own growth currently happens in bursts whenever things go quiet, this is the case for the small daily version instead.

## Guest

Bob Doyle is President and CEO of the Michigan Association of Certified Public Accountants (MICPA). He moved the association's entire education and training program into a virtual environment in 2020, something he says his members would have called impossible when he started. He still handwrites a journal entry every morning, and he trained his whole staff on active listening partly to force his own practice of it. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/bob-doyle-348b5b7/).

## Key ideas

**Growth behaves like a muscle, so it only develops if it is scheduled.** Doyle's version is the quiet time before the computer goes on - faith, reading, a journal entry, one note of gratitude - and his argument is that everyone finds their own outlet, but nobody grows without putting time against it.

**They are success skills, not soft skills.** Doyle's objection is that the label misrepresents the thing. Technical professionals already have mandated technical education; the non-technical side is what carries a career, and the name should say so.

**Gratitude has to include the thorns, not just the roses.** He writes down one specific thing from the previous day he was grateful for, and he deliberately includes what went badly, because that is where the growth is. Drost is honest that this is the hard version of the habit.

**When someone brings a leader a problem, the listening usually is the job.** Doyle calls it empathetic or reflective listening, and its tools are clarifying questions rather than answers. Most people are working through a limiting belief and reach their own solution once they are genuinely heard - which is what builds the trust underneath the relationship.

**Teaching a skill to your team is an effective way to install it in yourself.** Doyle ran an all-staff session on active listening - phone away, email off, body language - and says the reason he did it was that teaching something is how you learn it. Now the conversations themselves remind him.

**A disrupted year is a growth event, and the target is a new better rather than a new normal.** Doyle refuses the wish-this-year-away framing, citing a poem asking what if this was the year we needed. What his association proved it could do under duress is not something he intends to give back.

## Try this

- Block fifteen minutes and journal twice in the next seven days. Delete it afterwards if you want - Drost's point is that the value is in organizing the thoughts, not in keeping them.
- Tomorrow morning, before the laptop opens, write one line about something from yesterday you are grateful for. Do it again the day after, and make at least one entry this week about something that went badly.
- Pick the success skill you are worst at and put a 30-minute session on your team's calendar where you teach it. Doyle trained his whole staff on active listening for exactly this reason.
