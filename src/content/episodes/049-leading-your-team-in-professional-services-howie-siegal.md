## Why this episode

You get handed a proposal or a project and the instinct is to open the file and start. Howie Siegal argues that the first move is people, not work, because until you have collected several internal perspectives you are solving the problem you assumed rather than the one that exists. This is a step-by-step walkthrough of one project lifecycle — team, stakeholders, vision, alignment, timeline, feedback — for anyone who leads delivery on a client deadline.

## Guest

Howie Siegal is an audit partner with RSM and a returning guest; his first appearance argued that leadership is a behavior, not a title. He works proposals and RFPs across audit, tax and consulting lines, and describes preparing his own notes and point of view before he will call an internal expert. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/howardsiegal/).

## Key ideas

**The first move on a new project or RFP is building the team, not starting the work.** Siegal's reason is that the brief is daunting and incomplete on arrival, and you need several internal perspectives before you understand what is being asked. He also treats the team as fluid rather than a fixed three names, and says there are no bad reach-outs at that stage.

**Do the reconnaissance before you make the call, so every conversation is two-way.** He writes down his thoughts and his point of view before phoning an industry expert inside his own firm — his phrase is that he refuses to open with "give me your brain dump." By the time he reaches the client, he is asking pointed questions rather than "what's your five-year strategy?"

**A leader's job is to get the best outcome, not to be right, so convictions stay flexible until the team has argued about them.** Siegal is explicit that having a conviction is not evidence it is correct, and that drawing lines in the sand during the alignment phase is the mistake. Different people hear different things in the same client call, and reconciling those readings is the work.

**Inclusive leadership done badly is detectable, and it costs you more than disagreeing would.** Accepting a colleague's idea without probing it — because you have been told to include other people — is what Siegal calls hollow. His alternative is to ask what they heard that got them there. You earn more respect digging into an idea and mutually concluding it doesn't fit than by dropping it in and quietly deleting it at the end.

**The timeline is the most critical execution artifact, because it converts a project into accountability deadlines.** Set the drop-dead date first, then divide the work backward across days or weeks. Siegal's argument is that this is what makes quality possible — ASAP is not a timeline, and ambiguity about when something is due either stretches it forever or compresses it into chaos.

**Staffing someone on a task they will need coaching through is a legitimate use of the timeline's slack.** Siegal plans the extra hours in rather than routing around the gap. His position is that in professional services nothing you do should be free of some development of the team, and that the buy-in you get back is part of what gets the project delivered.

## Try this

- Take the next proposal that lands on your desk and, before you open the template, write down three internal people whose perspective you need and book the calls. Prepare your own notes and point of view for each one first.
- Pick a proposal you lost in the last year and call the buyer to ask why they chose someone else. Siegal did exactly this with a CFO and learned that his pricing page had confused them — a defect he could not see because his team had built it.
- On your current project, set the drop-dead delivery date and work backward into named accountability deadlines across the weeks between now and then. Then look for one task you can hand to someone who will need coaching, and book the coaching time into the same plan.
