## Why this episode

The news is bad, your calendar is a checkerboard, and telling yourself to be positive about it feels like denial. Carrie Schochet treats a positive mindset as a choice you make daily and then prop up with unglamorous habits - early quiet time, a short journal entry, a bike ride booked on the work calendar like any other appointment. If your response to all of that is that you do not have time, she has a direct answer for you.

## Guest

Carrie Schochet is founder and president of Purple Squirrel Advisors, an executive recruiting firm. She spent eleven years at a global recruiting firm in Chicago before moving back to Detroit seven years ago, where she had to rebuild a practice in a business community that works nothing like Chicago's. She is a runner and cyclist with three children under nine, and she is candid that sitting still is not how she gets a clear head. She is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/carrieschochetrecruiter/).

## Key ideas

**Positivity is a choice made daily against constant negative input, not a personality you were issued.** Schochet's framing is that social media, the news and the people around you are all pushing the other way, so the counterweight has to be deliberate and repeated.

**Journaling is measurement.** You cannot manage what you do not measure, and a journal is the only reliable instrument for your own thinking. Read what you wrote 30 days ago and the change is visible; skip the writing and you will simply forget how you felt.

**"I do not have time" is a statement about priorities, not about hours.** Schochet's rule is short: if it is important it goes on the calendar, and if it is not important it comes off the calendar. The fix for an overloaded week is subtraction, not compression.

**Remote work removed the seams in the day, so over-scheduling became the default.** Drive time and the semi-social lunch meeting used to create breaks nobody had to defend. Without them the day runs 9 to 5 on video, which Schochet names as exhausting rather than productive.

**Your attitude is an input to everyone else's day.** Positive energy is contagious - somebody smiles at you and you smile back - which makes your state other people's problem too. Her point is that you cannot bring energy you have not generated, which is what the habits are for.

**Resilience is what Schochet hires for, and she is not sure it can be taught.** She listens for candidates who can describe overcoming something. Drost pushes back: lived experience is required, but you can deliberately prepare yourself to handle bigger challenges before they arrive.

## Try this

- Open next week's calendar and find one recurring meeting you have never questioned. Demote it to a priority two or take it off, then put something that gives you energy in the empty slot and treat it as a real appointment.
- Write two sentences tomorrow morning before you open email: what has to happen today for you to feel good about the day. Do it for fourteen days, then go back and read the first entry.
- Find one person chasing the same habit and trade a daily prompt with them. Schochet's argument for it is specific - on the morning you would have skipped, somebody is waiting on you.
