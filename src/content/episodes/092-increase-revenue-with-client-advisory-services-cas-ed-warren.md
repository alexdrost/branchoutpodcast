## Why this episode

You want recurring advisory revenue, but you are still priced like a generalist and the work still runs out of your own head. Ed Warren walks through what a client advisory services practice actually demands: one industry you know cold, a process documented well enough that someone else can run it, and a willingness to hand back compliance work you have done for years in order to take the accounting work behind it. If you are weighing whether to build CAS, treat this as the operating checklist rather than the pitch.

## Guest

Ed Warren works with Sage Intacct, in a group focused on helping CPAs and accounting services providers grow their practices. He calls himself a recovering accountant — he started his career doing accounting for a Fortune 30 company — and has since spent over 25 years in professional service sales and marketing, including marketing, business development and practice growth roles at top 100 CPA firms. This episode walks through the seven items in his e-book on increasing revenue through client advisory services. [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/edwarddwarren/)

## Key ideas

**Advisory is forecasting; the compliance work is the rearview mirror.** Warren's line between the two is concrete: a cash flow calendar, a dashboard of what is coming in and going out over the next 90 days, and a view on whether to buy capital equipment this year or next. The close still has to happen. It is just no longer the product.

**If you only work as a generalist, you can only charge generalist prices.** Warren's analogy is bypass surgery — a general surgeon could do it in a pinch, and you would still want the specialist who does hearts three days a week. His related point is that clients assume the letters after your name mean you understand their business, and they are usually wrong.

**A marginal audit relationship can be worth more given away than kept.** Independence rules mean you cannot audit the books you prepare, so Warren tells firms with weak, low-margin audit clients to drop the audit and take over the accounting instead: potentially five to ten times the revenue, at better margin, and more use to the client.

**A retiring controller is a specific trigger event, not a general opportunity.** His example is a 72-year-old who had spent 20 years in the Army, come back, earned a CPA and run an association's accounting — and who treated outsourcing the function as his own exit strategy. The firm picked up the work when he retired.

**Fractional work has no social credit to draw on.** You are not in the office, not in the football pool, not bringing a dish to the picnic. Warren's point is that a fractional provider is judged on work product alone, which raises the bar on both client experience and staff quality.

**Recurring revenue scales through documented process, not through stacking bodies.** Traditional leverage means adding people you bill out above what you pay them; a CAS practice has effectively fixed recurring revenue, so growth comes from efficiency. Warren invokes the E-Myth standard — build the model so someone with a sixth-grade education could execute it — and argues a growth specialist and then a COO should be among your first senior hires, because the people who build these practices are rarely the ones who make the trains run on time.

## Try this

- Pick the one industry you could describe three current trends in without looking anything up, then find the two conferences its owners actually attend and submit a speaking proposal to one of them this quarter.
- Pull your audit list and flag every engagement where the margin is thin and the client's financials are weak. For the worst one, price what ongoing accounting work for that client would be worth, and take the comparison to whoever owns the relationship.
- Document one recurring process end to end this week — the monthly close for a single client — at a level of detail a new hire could follow on their own by Friday.
