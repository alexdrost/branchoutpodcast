## Why this episode

You have been told to network for business development, which is exactly why you keep not doing it. Dillon Zwick, a self-described introvert who reached management consulting by way of biochemistry, history and industrial design, argues the returns you can actually feel are job security, real friendship, and the ninety percent of what your field knows that nobody ever writes down. If you tried a few events, disliked them and concluded this is not for you, his answer is that you stopped in the middle of the filtering.

## Guest

Dillon Zwick does management consulting at MeadowLark Advisors, a firm of about eight to ten people in Austin, Texas. He studied biochemistry and history, moved through industrial design and several years at a manufacturing firm, and says he would never have gotten through the front door of his current field on his resume. He wrote the article on why professionals should be networking that prompted this conversation. Find him at [linkedin.com/in/dillon-zwick](https://www.linkedin.com/in/dillon-zwick/).

## Key ideas

**Technical ability caps out two or three years into a career, and everything after that is interpersonal.** Nobody is made managing partner for being technically sound. Zwick's version of advancement is recruiting people to work on a problem with you, which means networking above you, beside you, and behind you so someone can fill your seat when you move.

**Job security now means always knowing you have another job.** Zwick got the definition from a mentor who had watched companies post record profits one year and fold the next. His test is concrete: if things went sideways tomorrow, he could make a handful of calls and have interviews the next day.

**In a firm of eight or ten people there is no hiring manager, just a question asked at a meeting.** Postings draw a hundred near-identical resumes and get filtered on arbitrary things like a spelling mistake. Small middle-market firms without HR simply ask their own people who they know, which is the door Zwick himself came through.

**Roughly ninety percent of what a field knows is never recorded anywhere.** One or two people in any discipline write, speak or tweet; the practitioners closest to the problems are the ones who do not publish, and they hold the most current picture. Zwick offers the split as his own heuristic rather than a measured fact.

**Networking is a legitimate route back to friendship, not a substitute for it.** He cites millennials as the loneliest generation, with roughly one close friend against their great-grandparents' four, and points out that friendships have natural life cycles. Lose them without making new ones and the arithmetic ends where you would expect.

**Expect four to six months in the wrong rooms before you find yours.** Zwick worked through Eventbrite listings across technology, business, coffees and after-hours drinks before landing on ACG. The discomfort of the early conversations is the price of the later ones, not evidence you are bad at this.

## Try this

- Book three conversations in the next two weeks: one with someone above you, one at your level, one earlier in their career than you. The third is the one everyone skips and the one who backfills your seat.
- Start a standing monthly call with four or five people in your field and use it strictly for what each of you is seeing in the market. Send the first invitation this week rather than waiting for a reason.
- If your last networking group did not fit, try two more before deciding networking is not for you. Zwick went through Eventbrite listings for four or five months before ACG worked.
