## Why this episode

You have said it in a client meeting: time kills all deals. Denise Logan's response is that whoever says it is the most frightened person in the room, and that what actually kills deals is emotion nobody has dealt with. Alex Drost, a former investment banker, admits on air to having used the line — so you get the industry's central maxim taken apart with one of its users in the room, and a replacement claim that slowing down closes more transactions rather than fewer.

## Guest

Denise Logan was a mental health professional, then a lawyer who built a law practice, burned out and exited it. For the last 13 years she has worked one on one with business owners and their advisors on the emotional side of letting go of a business; her tagline is The Seller Whisperer. She is the author of The Seller's Journey, a business fable about the obstacles owners hit while selling, and she speaks at around 70 conferences a year. [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/deniselogan/)

## Key ideas

**"Time kills all deals" is a confession, not a diagnosis.** Logan hears it as the sound of someone frightened that something outside their control is about to spin out. She said as much to a room of bankers whose process commits sellers to a fixed number of days to close, and watched the founder's face go red — which is, in her reading, the argument proving itself.

**A deal accumulates anxiety the way Chutes and Ladders does.** Every setback spikes everyone's emotion, and if it is not settled before the next move, the level creeps until someone wants to flip the board and leave the room. The banker in the room jumped straight to whether they were supposed to wait two years for a deal to close. Her answer: the delay you are refusing is usually twelve minutes, or a day and a half.

**For the owner it is a transition; for you it is a transaction.** This is the single largest transition of their adult professional life and your next closed file, and the mismatch is where deals break. Run the mandate as throughput — days to close, efficiency, push it through — and you produce the seller's remorse you were trying to outrun.

**If you are talking sense and getting nonsense back, emotion is already in the room.** Logan's claim is that advisors are often traumatized by emotion in past transactions, so they try to squelch all of it — their own, the client's, opposing counsel's. Lawyers arguing endlessly about something that makes no sense is the same signal.

**Low close rates are manufactured by the response to low close rates.** About 30% of companies that come to market successfully transition, so advisors take on more deals, invest less in each, force the ones that stall, and reproduce the failures that justified the volume. Her prescription runs the other way: attend to fewer deals properly, and the seller on the other side becomes the best referral source you will get.

**Growth deserves the question "better for who."** Logan's own happiest stretch was 600 square feet with a paralegal and a legal assistant, before growth became a hungry machine she had to keep feeding. Her replacement frame is product-market-maker fit — put yourself back in the equation, and you can right-size the business rather than default to bigger.

## Try this

- The next time a call turns strange — you are making sense and getting nonsense back — call a break instead of saying it again louder. Logan manufactures one by claiming she needs the restroom. Let the room come down before you return to the agenda.
- Put one conversation with no deal content in it on the calendar before your next sell-side kickoff: ask the owner what their Monday looks like the week after closing, and write down what they say where the deal team can see it.
- Count the live mandates you are carrying and name the one you have not properly attended to in a month. Decide this week whether to resource it or hand it back, instead of carrying every deal at the same shallow depth.
