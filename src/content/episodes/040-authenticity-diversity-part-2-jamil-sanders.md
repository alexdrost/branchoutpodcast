## Why this episode

You want to build relationships across real difference and you keep running into the same wall: the conversations stay polite and nothing moves. Jamil Sanders argues the blockage is upstream of the other person. The empathy, grace and actual listening you can offer someone unlike you is capped by how much of it you extend to yourself, and this episode is about what that ceiling looks like in practice.

## Guest

Jamil Sanders is Vice President and Relationship Manager with Key Private Bank in Cleveland, Ohio. He is a returning guest — this is the unplanned second half of a conversation that ran long, and he draws on his high school wrestling career, a senior who told him he would never make state, and the sixth-place medal he keeps in the house to make the argument. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/jamil-a-sanders/).

## Key ideas

**Sanders' central claim is a ceiling, not an encouragement: you cannot be empathetic to others past the point you are empathetic to yourself.** He frames it as self-love and he means it structurally. If you cannot give yourself grace for a bad day, the grace you offer someone else is performance, and it runs out at exactly the moment it is needed.

**Whatever internal programming you carry gets projected outward without you noticing.** His example is his own: because he had decided nobody cared about his ideas, he half-listened to his wife for years while insisting he heard her. He is careful to say he was not ignoring her — he was hearing the words and not processing them, because his own baseline told him words like that don't matter.

**The question that fixes listening is asked of yourself, in the moment: am I listening to listen, or listening to understand?** Sanders' point is that "you're not listening to me" is never a claim about your ears. When someone says it, the useful response is to ask what you are missing, not to prove that the sound arrived.

**Success is a direction, not a mile marker, and treating a goal as the definition of success is what produces the arrogance-then-collapse cycle.** He went on a twenty-match tear, got told he could place at state, stopped preparing the way he had been, and got dominated at the Brentwood Classic. His diagnosis is not that he got lazy — it is that hitting the marker made him forget his why.

**Diversity and inclusion progress is incremental and cannot be hypercharged into a matter of months.** Asked directly what a white colleague should do, Sanders declines to give a program with a completion date. The frame he offers instead is a journey you enjoy and keep learning inside of, which is a slower answer than most listeners want and is deliberately so.

**Cross-difference relationships are built by asking what someone does like and then inviting them into your personal space.** Not what they don't like, not where their views fail to line up with yours. And not lunch — lunch is still a transaction. Sanders means your family, your cookout, the space you would not normally open to a professional contact.

## Try this

- Next time someone tells you they don't feel heard, say "help me understand what I'm missing" instead of confirming that you heard the words. Sanders' whole listening argument turns on that one substitution.
- Pick one person in your network whose views you know don't line up with yours and ask them what they actually like — music, food, weekends. Do not raise the disagreement. The goal is to find the overlap, not to litigate the gap.
- Take Alex Drost's call to action: within seven days, revisit one situation where you reacted badly, give yourself grace for it, and decide specifically how you will react next time.
