## Why this episode

Your website says full service, and the last three proposals you lost came down to fee. Mike Jones has worked in brand strategy for close to twenty years and is midway through a hand-built study of more than 1,500 accounting firm brands, and his finding is that breadth is what pushes a firm into the price fight. If you want a buyer to choose you before they compare capabilities, this is where that comes from.

## Guest

Mike Jones has worked in brand and brand strategy for almost his entire career, roughly twenty years, and runs a ten-person agency whose clients are largely B2B professional services firms. He and his team are manually collecting data on just over 1,500 accounting firm brands, testing whether the most specifically positioned firms are the ones winning. He hosts the Remarkabrand Podcast and wrote the book You Are Remarkable. [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/remarkamike/)

## Key ideas

**Brand is the shortcut a buyer's brain takes to avoid spending energy on a decision.** Jones asks audiences to pick between a Volkswagen and a Chrysler minivan and about 80% choose the Volkswagen for styling, German engineering or safety. The Volkswagen in that photo is a licensed Chrysler with different sheet metal, sold for about ten thousand dollars more. The premium is the brand.

**"Full service" is a cop-out, and it delivers you into a price comparison.** Jones's read is that firms say it because they do not want to turn anyone away. The cost is that when three proposals look alike, the decision resolves to fee or to who somebody happens to know — and, he argues, staking a business on individual relationships is fragile when you do not know who will still be at the firm in five years.

**Full service works when it is full service for one kind of business.** Breadth becomes credible the moment it is scoped: full service for e-commerce companies is an expertise claim, full service for everyone is not. Jones's version of this is a target with a bullseye — you will still take the client slightly off-center, but you never aim there and you never market there.

**Geographic niching is dying, and hyper-local only works if you go all the way.** For decades accounting and law firms were defined by the market they could drive across. Jones, shopping nationally for a firm to handle his own company's filings in three states, says he does not care where they sit. A firm that still claims hyper-local has to prove it on every page — down to whether staff are allowed to live out of state.

**In a service firm your people are the delivery mechanism, and the biggest untapped gap is what they say.** Ask five people at the same firm what it does and you get five answers, partners included. Jones's fix is a single defined story — hero, problem, guide, solution, and the change it produces, with the client as the hero — that everyone is trained to tell in their own words rather than improvising.

**Build the brand from the inside out.** Starting from market gaps produces trend-chasing and claims your people cannot back up, which Jones calls a lie even when it is a wishful one. Study the market and the competition, but define identity from what the firm is actually strong at.

## Try this

- Ask five people across your firm, separately and this week, "what does the firm do?" Write down all five answers. The spread between them is the gap Jones is describing.
- Draft your firm's story on one page using his five pieces — hero, problem, guide, solution, and the change it produces — with the client as the hero. Then narrow the hero until it names one industry or one problem set, and cut the rest of the messaging to match.
- If one person in your firm is the public face, pick their successor now and put both of them in every post, panel and podcast for the next 18 months. Announcing the handover in a press release does not transfer the recognition.
