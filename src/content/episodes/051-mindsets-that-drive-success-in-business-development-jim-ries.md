## Why this episode

You go to the events, you collect the cards, and then nothing happens. Jim Ries, a non-lawyer running business development inside a 250-attorney firm, points at the boxes and drawers of uncontacted business cards at his own firm and says that gap is the whole opportunity: follow up at all and you are ahead of most of your field. If you want the calendar mechanics rather than another argument for being helpful, he walks through how his week is actually built.

## Guest

Jim Ries is Director of Business Development with Offit Kurman, a full-service law firm with roughly 250 attorneys across 41 practice areas, in 14 offices in seven states. He spent 31 years in a family shoe business before moving into professional services, worked in a management role at the firm for about four years, and was moved into business development full time when the firm saw where he was excelling. He also hosts Java With Jim and says he makes about three introductions a day. Find him at [linkedin.com/in/jries](https://www.linkedin.com/in/jries/).

## Key ideas

**Speak publicly only in the lane your network already credits you with.** Ries asks what his network expects to hear from him and answers connecting, business development and networking. A non-attorney posting as a subject matter expert on international tax law spends credibility instead of building it.

**The fake giver does more damage than the taker.** Ries's taxonomy runs takers, genuine givers who expect nothing back, and in the middle the people who ask how they can help and then never do it. The failure sits entirely in the follow-through, which is what makes it read as inauthentic rather than merely busy.

**Follow-up is the cheapest competitive advantage available in professional services.** His own firm's uncontacted business cards are the proof, and his standard is delivering what he promised inside 24 hours. He also notes most people who fail at it are not making empty promises; they are just letting things fall through.

**Networking admin belongs on the calendar as a block, not in the gaps.** Alex Drost's structure is 15 minutes to an hour a week for follow-ups, promised introductions, CRM entry and contact notes. Ries's addition is that anything on a calendar gets treated like a meeting, so it gets done.

**A 30-minute call does the work of a two-hour coffee, which is why he defaults first meetings to video or phone.** He reserves Fridays for in-person coffee and lunch, keeps meetings out of the after-5 window so that time can absorb email and follow-ups, and screens evening events hard against travel time and family.

**A portable book of business is job security in both directions.** Offit Kurman gives every attorney a starting point and a roadmap of benchmarks, and expects billable hours to give way as the book grows. Ries adds the detail that lawyers uniquely have no non-competes, so a book travels with them.

## Try this

- Block 30 minutes this week, sort your inbox oldest first, and clear the follow-ups you promised. Ries's addition is to start with the message you have been avoiding, because that is the one that keeps sinking.
- Put a recurring weekly block on your calendar for networking admin, anywhere from 15 minutes to an hour, and use it only for promised introductions, contact notes and CRM entry.
- Pick one weekday and take meetings off it entirely for a month. Ries uses Friday for concentration work specifically so the work stops migrating into his weekend.
