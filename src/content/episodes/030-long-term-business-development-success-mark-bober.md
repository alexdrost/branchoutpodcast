## Why this episode

You are being asked to bring in work, and everything you have been shown about how to do it looks like selling. This episode is for the technical professional — the accountant, the lawyer, the analyst — who suspects they are bad at business development because they are not the person working the room. Mark Bober built a large practice over 35 years without ever doing traditional prospecting, and is specific about what he did instead.

## Guest

Mark Bober is a partner with BMF, an Ohio-based full-service CPA and advisory firm, where he leads the transactions and M&A practice and the litigation and expert witness practice. He describes himself as an introvert who never tried to emulate an extroverted personality and became a top producer at his firm anyway. He is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/mark-bober-1a46631/).

## Key ideas

**Business development is a marathon, and most compensation plans are built for a sprint.** Bober's core complaint is structural: incentive compensation keyed to results generated this year sits badly against a seed-planting cycle where only some seeds sprout in a given year. People come back from meetings asking what was supposed to happen, and the honest answer is that it may take years.

**Bober attributes his own practice to delivery, not to selling.** He focused on technical expertise, industry knowledge and execution on projects — explicitly not the outward-facing part — and let satisfied clients refer him. His counterexample is the professional who lands work and then under-serves it because they are already chasing the next lead, and ends up permanently hunting cold.

**The other advisors on your deal are a referral channel you are not trying to build.** The attorneys, investment bankers and consultants across the table watch how you work, and the call arrives years later from someone you never targeted. Alex Drost supplies the inverse from his banking years: the providers who were unpleasant to work with are the ones he quietly steered people away from.

**Introversion is not the barrier. The comfort zone is.** Bober says you do not need "wow" as your top StrengthsFinder theme to build a practice, and that trying to emulate an extrovert instead of being authentic is the wrong fix. What is required is being willing to step outside your comfort zone, which is a different demand than changing your personality.

**Price a client the way Apple prices a customer.** Bober imports lifetime value and net promoter thinking from consumer companies: the question is not what this engagement is worth but what the relationship is worth, and whether the client comes back for the next thing.

**Growth is not optional once you are past sole practitioner.** Bober's argument is a talent argument. If you cannot create new work, you cannot create advancement, and the people you spent years recruiting leave.

## Try this

- Treat your next networking meeting as a research call. Leave with one specific thing about that person's industry you can use with an existing client this month, and use it.
- Look at next week's inbound meeting requests and decline the ones outside your niche — in writing, with a referral to someone better suited. Bober's constraint is that the hours are finite, and the referral costs you nothing.
- For one week, every time you catch yourself saying "I don't have time for that," restate it as "that's not a priority for me." Then check whether the real obstacle is the calendar or the discomfort.
