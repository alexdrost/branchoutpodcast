## Why this episode

You are one of many firms telling the same bankers the same things, and you are losing on things that have nothing to do with price. This episode is for anyone whose pipeline depends on other people choosing to call them back — business development, origination, or a client-facing seat where the next engagement comes from the last one. Jessica Ginsberg's answers are unusually operational: an inbox procedure, a headcount screen, and a deal her firm nearly lost by being the smartest people in the room.

## Guest

Jessica Ginsberg is director of business development for LFM Capital, a lower-middle-market private equity firm based in Nashville. Three of the firm's four partners came through MIT's Leaders for Global Operations program and out of operating roles at companies including Dell and Caterpillar, and the firm's offices are in an old firehouse. Ginsberg's job is to turn that operating history into deal flow. She is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/jessicalginsberg/).

## Key ideas

**Not responding to a banker's deal costs you the next one.** Ginsberg hears constantly from bankers who send opportunities to firms and get silence back. The banker stops assuming you are interested, and — her second point, delivered flatly — it is rude.

**A relationship practice needs an actual workflow.** LFM was founded by engineers steeped in lean manufacturing, and every drawer and shelf in the office kitchen is labeled with what belongs in it. Ginsberg's version for email: scan the last 24 hours each morning, open every message that requires a response, and leave it open until it is answered.

**A CRM tells you who to contact, not what to say.** Knowing that a thousand people have gone 90 days without contact is not a reason to send a thousand people the same message. Ginsberg treats the list as something to work through deliberately, one intended message at a time.

**Capital is commoditized, so the story has to be true.** Everyone's dollars look the same. LFM's differentiator is partners who grew up on shop floors and can talk credibly about the businesses they buy — and Ginsberg is direct that "another firm looking to make money" is not a story.

**Headcount belongs on the screen alongside revenue and EBITDA.** Ginsberg's firm hesitates above roughly 250 employees, because past that it becomes hard to know everybody's name. It is a people constraint used as a hard filter, not a preference.

**Chemistry runs ahead of competence in a management meeting.** Her team walked into a management dinner on a business they understood cold, opened with business questions to show it, and scored badly on cultural fit against bidders who just talked baseball. They took the banker's feedback, asked the seller for a redo in Nashville, and won the deal.

## Try this

- Go back through the deals you passed on this quarter and reply to each banker with two sentences on why. Then keep doing it in real time.
- Run Ginsberg's inbox procedure tomorrow: scan only the last 24 hours, open every message that needs a reply, and treat the open tabs as your list until each one is closed out.
- Before your next management meeting or pitch, agree with your team that the first fifteen minutes contain no business questions. Afterward, ask the banker directly how you scored on fit.
