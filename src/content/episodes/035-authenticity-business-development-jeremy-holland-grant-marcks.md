## Why this episode

You do business development by phone call number four hundred, not by phone call number one, and somewhere in there the performance starts to cost more than it returns. Jeremy Holland and Grant Marcks, colleagues at The Riverside Company, take apart what actually holds up over that many touch points — and what does not. If you have ever wondered whether the version of you that shows up on those calls is sustainable, this is the conversation about why it usually is not.

## Guest

Jeremy Holland is Managing Partner of Originations for The Riverside Company; Grant Marcks is a Principal at the same firm, recruited by Holland and now covering the Mid-Atlantic through New York. Holland started in private equity in the 1990s straight out of a state school with no MBA, no banking experience and no law degree, and spent years wondering whether people thought he belonged in the room. Both are returning guests from season one. Holland is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/jeremyrholland/); Marcks is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/grant-marcks-722bb671/).

## Key ideas

**Authenticity is consistency of behavior plus transparency, and Holland is explicit that it is not permission to behave badly.** He draws the line hard: not profane, not ill-behaved. The test he offers is whether you are the same person at a work function, in your backyard hosting a barbecue, and in front of your children as you are when they are not in the room.

**Consistency is measured in quality of interaction, not volume of touch points.** Marcks points out the real difficulty of the job: dozens of interpersonal exchanges a day, most of them delivering a no or a pass, each one still needing to be worth the other person's time. High frequency is the easy part.

**Saying out loud that you have no agenda is what makes people pick up the phone.** Marcks opens calls by stating there is nothing he needs and asking what he can do for them. He treats any deal that comes out of it as downstream and optional — his position is that the absence of expectation is the whole reason the call gets answered.

**You cannot be everything to everyone, so invest narrowly and exit politely from the rest.** Holland's rule is to follow your gut at events: if you do not click with someone, notice your cocktail is empty and move on. The time you save goes into the small group where a real friendship is possible, which is where he says the work stops feeling like work.

**The barrier to authenticity is insecurity, and the credential you are embarrassed about may be the asset.** Holland hid his state school degree for years. Then he found that self-made entrepreneurs — many of them told they were stupid before they built something — responded to the unpolished version of his background, and were tired of Harvard MBAs lecturing them about businesses they had never run.

**Real disclosure to an inner circle produces reciprocal disclosure, with a boundary.** Holland took calls from the car during a family health crisis and said so. What came back was other people's struggles, unprompted, and bonds that would not have formed otherwise. Alex Drost adds the limit: share at the right level, do not turn the call into dumping.

## Try this

- Take Alex Drost's call to action: in the next seven days, make two 15-minute check-in calls with no agenda at all. If they don't answer, leave a voicemail and send a text asking them to call you back.
- Replace "how are you?" on your next five calls with one specific question — whether they have gotten out to exercise, how they are finding a creative way to take a vacation. Holland's version starts by naming your own chaos first.
- Write down the part of your background you tend to leave out of the room. Then use it, once, in a meeting this month where you would normally lead with the polished version.
