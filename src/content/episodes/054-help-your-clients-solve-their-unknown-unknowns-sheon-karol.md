## Why this episode

You call yourself a trusted advisor on your website. Sheon Karol thinks you are probably not one, and his reason is not that you are dishonest — it is that you sell one thing, and the human mind gravitates to what it does every day. This episode is worth your time if you want the uncomfortable version of the specialization argument, and a workable answer to it that does not depend on anyone being more virtuous.

## Guest

Sheon Karol is a partner with Paladin, a middle-market advisory firm, and he started his career as a lawyer. He wrote the Entrepreneur article on unknown unknowns that prompted this conversation, and developed a framework he calls early signs of change, which he has presented to professional firms. He works extensively with lawyers and is unsparing about their client communications. Find him on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/sheon-karol-a0259914/).

## Key ideas

**Very few of the professionals who call themselves trusted advisors are one.** Karol's objection is structural: a professional focused on a single area pushes the client toward a single solution, and at a moment of dislocation what the client needs is a range of options. He questions the cliché directly rather than softening it.

**The bias is a heuristics problem, not a character problem.** Even an entirely moral advisor gravitates toward what they know and do every day, which Karol connects to Tversky and Kahneman on how our natural go-to moves deceive us. That is why he thinks the fix is compensation design rather than an appeal to integrity.

**You go to a GP before a surgeon for your body and straight to the surgeon for your business.** The surgeon cuts, so if you go to the surgeon there is a good chance you get cut. Karol's point is that people apply less sophistication to their company than they routinely apply to their own health.

**Firms should pay people for recommending work the firm does not perform.** At Paladin, Karol says the underlying thesis is a broad base of services with a financial incentive, beyond morality, to do what is best for the client. If you are only rewarded for what you personally do, that is a disincentive to give broad advice.

**Client alerts written for other lawyers are read by other lawyers.** Karol tells lawyers plainly that almost none of their clients read the alerts — they will not power through the legalese to reach a Delaware case they do not care about. The successful minority write about the client's supply chain instead.

**The highest-value move is getting ahead of the client, including on psychological signals.** A management team unchanged for many years is either the perfect team or a symptom of complacency, and the professional is often the one positioned to ask which. Karol pairs this with an admission most guests do not make: project confidence outwardly, hold humility inwardly, and keep re-examining your own advice.

## Try this

- Rewrite your next client alert so the first paragraph is about the client's supply chain, cost, or contracts. Cut the case name, the jurisdiction, and the hedging, and lead with a change you saw before they did.
- In an owner-run client whose management team has not changed in years, ask the question directly: perfect team, or complacency? Then ask whether it is time to sell, in whole or in part.
- Start a book this week that has nothing to do with your practice area. Karol's recommendation is Lawrence Freedman's *Strategy*; Alex's most recent was Bill Gates on climate change.
